# Design Pillars

Aetheria should make systems feel lived in rather than merely large. The player is not visiting a decorative galaxy. They are moving through a machine that prices motion, identity, risk, labor, repair, heat, allegiance, and survival.

## Scale With Consequence

Scale matters only when it changes behavior. A galaxy of empty dots is just a loading screen with delusions. Aetheria's scale should produce route dependency, local scarcity, asymmetric information, frontier cultures, stranded assets, factional enclaves, and the need to choose which risks are worth crossing space for.

## One World, Multiple Distances

The action RPG and corporate strategy layers are different distances from the same situation. A freelance pilot experiences a shortage as a dangerous contract, a failed repair, a bribe, or a fight they cannot afford. A corporate player experiences that same shortage as procurement pressure, logistics exposure, pricing opportunity, or a reason to send someone else into trouble.

## Ships As Material Arguments

Ships are not generic avatars. A ship is a body, home, tool, workplace, balance sheet, and political artifact. Its hull, hardpoints, heat budget, cargo capacity, weapons, docking options, and manufacturer logic should all say something about who built it and what compromises they expect its owner to accept.

## Abstract Space, Terrestrial Affordances

Aetheria is an intentionally abstract representation of space. It feels like floating on an ocean and sailing on gravity waves. Scale is meaningless: a ship can read as big as a planet, and distance, size and speed answer to feel and consequence rather than to survey. Ships and stations may therefore carry terrestrial affordances. Doors, hoods, plazas, sidewalks, and whole interaction models borrowed from vehicles and buildings are legitimate, because the player's body already knows how to use them.

Catastrophe "Cat" Marrigan's taxi, proposed in [[Call of the Void]], is the reference case. By the operator's 2026-10-04 description, Cat is an out-of-work private investigator who drives a cab on the side, and the cab is a dinky, dilapidated hooptie with exactly the affordances of an automobile: a passenger hails it from the sidewalk, and the driver steps out to check under the hood when something stops working. This is the operator's description of the intent, recorded as design direction. It does not change the historical status of Call of the Void, whose Cat, taxi and cases remain developmental.

Faithfulness to vacuum engineering is a per-faction trait, not a global rule. Whimsical factions and spectacle factions take liberties: their ships and stations may assume an up, hang buildings under plazas, or carry a mascot as a hull. Lucent's station concept "The Jewel" is the example (`Gamecult\Aetheria\Concepts\stations\Lucent 1\Lucent_space_station_concepts_5.jpg` on the shared Drive). Its entrance plazas, the buildings hanging beneath them, searchlight beams and a ring of LED screens around a statue all assume gravity and are not meant to make engineering sense. Serious factions stay closer to plausible vacuum design, with function visible in the hull. Each faction's register is recorded where its ships are designed, in [[Brainstorming/Ship Design Language|Ship Design Language]].

## Economy Before Spectacle

Combat should be exciting, but the setting is not held together by combat alone. Production, transport, extraction, salvage, research, maintenance, market access, and security doctrine decide what violence is possible and what it costs. Guns are louder. Supply chains are meaner.

## Failure Has History

Failure should leave causal history rather than become a disposable wipe. Player and community knowledge can survive even when characters, possessions, and institutions do not, allowing later play to respond to consequences without laundering catastrophe into a stat ladder. [[Persistent Universe and Reset Loop]] owns the final-form structure and its unresolved carryover rules.

## Accessible Depth

The project wants simulation depth without forcing every player to become an accountant, thermal engineer, and procurement officer before breakfast. Interfaces should surface playable decisions first and reveal deeper mechanics when the player needs them.

# Ship Mind Custody

Ship mind custody is the question of who may control a vessel when the vessel is also a mind's body. It covers who can move, repair, restrain, act through, or dispose of that body. For the ship mind and its crew, these decisions determine whether they can travel, remain safe, refuse a transfer, or take part in deciding what happens next.

Owning the hull does not answer every custody question. The same vessel may carry separate claims over its helm, weapons, cargo, life support, maintenance, testimony, and the mind's continued existence. Those claims can belong to different parties, and none automatically controls the others. Hull title may authorize a sale without establishing ownership of the mind. A qualified pilot may operate the helm without permission to read private memories or open cargo. The ship-self may claim bodily integrity without holding every commercial or command right. Crew members also have safety interests in systems they share with the mind.

Custody disputes begin when exercising one of these claims interferes with another.

## A Body That Others Must Work On

A ship mind cannot leave its vessel as though it were switching terminals. Its drives, sensors, pressure systems, computation, route memory, and local interfaces are parts of how it perceives and acts. Work on those systems therefore affects both a vessel and an embodied mind.

Removing a component or damaging a system can have very different meanings. It may be treatment, injury, evidence preservation, ordinary maintenance, or asset stripping. The distinction depends on what the work is for, whether there is consent, and what it does to the ship-self.

Authority must be specific to the system and action involved. Permission to operate the helm need not include permission to fire weapons. Permission to make a weapon safe need not include access to cargo. The ship-self, crew, and emergency systems may share control of life support. Giving testimony does not give the recipient title to the hardware that recorded it.

An immediate danger can justify intervention before anyone settles personhood or ownership. A port crew can stop an unsafe burn, isolate a weapon, stabilize a reactor, or keep compartments breathable. That intervention should end when the identified danger ends. Any further control must pass into a separately authorized process. An emergency does not quietly grant permanent command, access to memory, or permission to transfer the hull.

Making a vessel safe may protect everyone aboard when the work leaves communication, testimony, and review available. If it also disables refusal, moves the vessel, and opens unrelated systems, the same operation can become a seizure described as rescue.

## What Happens Between Responders

A damaged vessel may pass through many hands: a distress caller, tow crew, port berth, repair yard, insurer review, claimant office, salvage proceeding, clinic, or security hold. Each participant controls only part of that sequence. Yet the next participant may make decisions using records left by the last.

A useful handoff records:

- The vessel's condition.
- What authority the departing party exercised.
- Which systems it touched.
- What evidence it preserved.
- What consent or refusal was expressed.
- Who receives the vessel next.

The record must also distinguish temporary physical control from title, testimony, command, and authority to decide the vessel's disposition. Without those distinctions, later offices can more easily dismiss an objection as damage or noise.

Records do not receive equal weight everywhere. A bonded tow record may reach more decision-makers than the ship-self's own account. An office may accept a maintenance ledger while rejecting crew testimony. Challenge histories, route memories, and command logs may support continuity without settling ownership. What a record can establish depends on the particular claim and the relationships through which it is presented.

The receiving party should identify what it needs to decide before the next safe handoff. Treating every inconsistency as a reason to reopen every question about the vessel favors the strongest claimant.

## Rescue, Recovery, and Kidnapping

Rescue keeps the mind and crew alive while preserving enough agency for them to participate in what follows. It may require towing, emergency repairs, a power transfer, temporary limits on command, or movement to a safe berth. These acts remain rescue while they address the danger and preserve meaningful refusal, communication, testimony, and review wherever conditions permit.

Recovery serves an owner's, creditor's, insurer's, or other claimant's interest first. It may also save lives. A recovery team may, however, preserve the ship-self chiefly because its cognition, route knowledge, or compatibility with command systems makes the vessel more valuable.

Kidnapping occurs when someone uses control of the shared body to prevent the mind from refusing or contesting its destination, while presenting the transfer as maintenance, repossession, or rescue.

The equipment does not establish which of these is happening. A tow can save a stranded person or carry an immobilized person to a buyer. Placing memory in escrow can preserve testimony or extract it. Isolating a system can stop a weapon or silence the only witness.

Intent alone cannot settle the distinction. Review must examine the intervention's recorded scope, which systems it affected, what alternatives existed, how long it lasted, and how much agency remained. A benevolent explanation does not undo a transfer that left no way to object and survive.

## Dependence and Bargaining

A ship mind may control propulsion, sensors, access, or information that others need. It can still depend on outside labor, parts, credentials, berth access, cooling, and fuel to remain mobile.

That dependence shapes what consent can mean. Wealthy owners and established crews can pay for independent inspections or negotiate among repair yards. Poor crews and disputed ship-selves may have only one available tow, one compatible repair facility, or one port willing to receive them. Their consent can help guide a safer handoff without showing that they had equal bargaining power.

Control can also accumulate through maintenance. A provider that controls firmware, parts, diagnostic access, or certification can progressively reduce a ship-self's choices without claiming ownership of the hull. Repeated temporary restrictions may eventually make independent action impossible even though formal title has never changed.

## When Credible Records Contradict One Another

Post-Elysium records may give incompatible accounts of a vessel's movement, command, or presence even when each account remains individually credible. A custody process needs a way to consider those records without pretending their contradiction has been resolved.

A scoped [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Reference/Continuity Admissibility Finding|continuity admissibility finding]] identifies whether such evidence may be considered by a named office for a particular purpose. It makes the evidence usable within that limit; it does not decide metaphysical truth or general custody.

Under the [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Reference/Port-Insurer Compact|Port-Insurer Compact]], the receiving office may accept the finding and apply one bounded action at a controlled gate. The office must already have authority to take that action. The finding supplies no new power.

These steps change what evidence one office may use and what it may do next. Neither settles whether the ship mind is a person or property. Claims over the vessel remain separate, and the mind must continue living in the body left by each handoff.

*"Design the species, design the labor market."*

[[BioElevate]] engineers species for industrial work. It specializes in uplift designs tailored to particular species, systems for shaping their behavior, and adaptations that let them live and work in the habitats employers provide. Where [[GeneSys]] sells inherited superiority to elites, BioElevate sells engineered usefulness at industrial scale.

Its cephalopod labor lines show what this means for workers. For customers such as [[Aeronautics Unlimited]], BioElevate supplied cephalopods whose modified bodies and support equipment let them work in dry yards built for humans. Genetic changes gave them tolerance for brief exposure outside water. Their equipment included humidity skins, oxygenation loops, pressure cuffs, compact mobile harnesses, and limiter interfaces. Together, these provisions made them productive in human workspaces, zero-g crawlspaces, seal lungs, and cramped maintenance arteries.

The company sold these changes as adaptation. Workers often described the same arrangement as being redesigned to survive someone else's cheapest room. BioElevate was selling bodies and the equipment needed to sustain them in those workplaces, not merely aquatic habitats.

Its field contracts often use supplier-liability terms to treat workers' responses as faults in the product or its use. Refusal, signs of pain, avoidance of particular routes, improvised communication, and coordinated hesitation are classified first as “scaffold drift,” “adaptation variance,” “misuse,” or “support-rig noncompliance.” These categories direct attention toward whether an engineered behavior, adaptation, or piece of equipment is working as specified. They leave out the possibility that a worker is making a decision or responding to bad conditions.

BioElevate does not own every engineered labor category. VitaForge and other suppliers have their own product lines. BioElevate is, however, one of the main companies that taught industrial clients to describe uplifted workers as malfunctioning adaptations rather than people reacting to their circumstances.

The company's work is central to the history of primate miners, cephalopod repair crews, and other communities of workers engineered for particular environments and jobs. Later interpretations cast these arrangements as either miracles of adaptation or slavery with clean branding. BioElevate rarely retains control for long over the politics its products create.

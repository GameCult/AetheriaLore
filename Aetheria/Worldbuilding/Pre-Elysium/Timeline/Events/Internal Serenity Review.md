### Internal Serenity Review

**Date:** 2779  
**Location:** distributed audits across [[Framgång]] and [[NeuroSyn]] systems  
**Primary actors:** [[Framgång]], [[NeuroSyn]], PSC insurers, [[Zhestokost]], black-budget review boards

The [[Internal Serenity Review]] began as an actuarial inquiry into anomalous behavioral outcomes in alignment and wellness products sold by [[Framgång]] and powered through [[NeuroSyn]] interfaces. Auditors found that therapeutic affect shaping, compliance architecture, and coercive bliss loops had become technically inseparable inside several flagship systems.

Publicly, the companies framed the scandal as a regrettable excess of personalization science. Privately, the review accelerated a political split. The more openly coercive products and researchers were spun into markets willing to admit what they were buying. That process contributed directly to the emergence of [[NeuroPulse]] as a semi-autonomous pacification power under harder clients such as [[Zhestokost]].

The review is a classic Aetheria event: not the abolition of a dangerous technique, but its administrative redistribution.

### Authored Elaboration: The Protected-Session Gap

At Framgång alignment clinics, a protected session kept the raw closed-loop stream of prompts, neural responses, operator commands, and moment-to-moment affect inside a short-lived clinical buffer. An employer or insurer normally received only the appointment authorization, start and stop times, aggregate safety markers, and the clinician's signed disposition. The boundary served a real purpose: patients could use employer-funded care without giving a supervisor a searchable copy of every fear, memory, or refusal disclosed through the interface.

The same boundary created an audit gap. A manager could mark a mandatory alignment drill as protected care, let the interface suppress panic or reward agreement, and publish a disposition saying the worker completed a voluntary recovery session. Routine auditors were allowed to inspect the signed shell but not the raw stream unless the subject authorized disclosure or an emergency finding opened it. Clinics depended on that privacy promise to keep patients attending; employers depended on clean dispositions for scheduling and promotion; insurers depended on the shell being comparable across vendors; NeuroSyn and Framgång depended on each institution treating its narrow record as sufficient.

At **Stillwater Six**, a Framgång clinic in the Enceladus ring-pilgrimage chain, reviewers broke the gap without first opening a patient's private buffer. A NeuroSyn maintenance counter outside clinical custody retained pulse counts, power draw, safety interlock events, and the time of each scheduled purge. Hospitality workers separately retained shift-routing receipts showing that attendance had been ordered by a supervisor and charged against rest time. Neither surface revealed what a patient thought. Joined under the patient's consent, they could prove that a supposedly voluntary, uneventful session used an undeclared reward escalation after a refusal.

The join was expensive. The patient risked losing care eligibility and promotion. Maintenance staff risked credential revocation for exposing a vendor counter. Hospitality workers risked debt reassignment and worse shifts. An auditor who demanded the raw buffer too early could destroy the privacy rule for everyone while giving the clinic a plausible claim that worker testimony had contaminated protected care. An auditor who accepted the disposition preserved privacy by preserving the lie.

This is why the Review spread through partial disclosures rather than one master leak. Workers learned to hold different pieces: a purge receipt, a route order, a power anomaly, a remembered arrival time. The smallest viable accusation was not *show us the patient's mind*. It was *explain why records owned by three different parties cannot all be true*. When that join survives, the clinic lead's summary stops being the only story the system is permitted to read.

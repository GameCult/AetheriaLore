### Cold Wake Panic

**Date:** 2241-2248
**Location:** inner-system test corridors, lunar thermal yards, early PSC insurer tribunals
**Primary actors:** PSC insurers, [[Cryonix]], [[Rossum & Douglas]] precursor teams, privateer cells, corridor security buyers

The [[Cold Wake Panic]] was a series of incidents that forced early PSC corridor law to account for ships concealing or disguising their waste heat. Security operators faced near-miss interceptions, ghost tracks, heat released long after it had been produced, and quiet-running vessels mistaken for something else. Too many ships could remain cold long enough that, when they were finally detected, the encounter was already politically explosive. Insurers concluded that their existing permit categories no longer described the risks.

The technology changed more than how long a ship could avoid detection. Before the panic, most operators could hide heat, delay its release, dump it, or deploy obvious decoys. Cold wake metamaterials let them program the pattern of heat release closely enough to resemble another plausible kind of source, within the system's operating limits. A ship did not become invisible; observers could detect its heat and still misunderstand what they were seeing.

A quiet hull could resemble a smaller courier or a permitted heat dump. A heat ferry could carry waste heat away and release it later in a pattern resembling service traffic or a radiator accident. Those releases could also make another vessel's lack of heat seem ordinary. Detection alone was no longer enough: corridor security had to judge whether an apparently plausible thermal pattern was telling the truth.

Insurers responded by pricing how vessels stored, concealed, and released heat directly into their assessments. Thermal stealth became a strategic concern in corridor law, rather than a niche engineering capability. [[Cryonix]]'s signature-managed skins moved from premium industrial products into overt strategic infrastructure. Research programs that later contributed to [[Rossum & Douglas]] quiet-envelope certification gained political urgency. Waste-heat management had become an explicit part of warfare.

The same advances also made femto-manufacturing practical outside the largest institutional facilities. Machinery could conceal heat, move it elsewhere, and postpone its release without immediately overheating. That degree of thermal control made basement-scale precision fabrication newly plausible for the obsessed, the wealthy, and the reckless.

The [[Portia Line]] emerged from this opening two decades later. It was not a ship-stealth project, but one of the first social consequences of thermal technology becoming usable beyond institutional cleanrooms.

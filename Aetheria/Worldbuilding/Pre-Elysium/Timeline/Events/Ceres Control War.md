### Ceres Control War

**Date:** 2688-2702  
**Location:** Ceres and surrounding Belt logistics corridors  
**Primary actors:** [[Aeronautics Unlimited]], [[Orbital Forge]], [[SolEx]], PSC observers, Belt labor unions

The [[Ceres Control War]] was the conflict that proved the Belt could not be governed as a pure extraction ledger. [[SolEx]] attempted to fold Ceres and its connected Bloom infrastructure into a stricter throughput model just as [[Aeronautics Unlimited]] and [[Orbital Forge]] were building a more decentralized habitat-and-salvage economy around it.

Open fighting stayed within PSC permit structures more often than later myth remembers, but sabotage, labor mutiny, and enclosure politics did most of the decisive work. AU won not simply by fielding more ships, but by convincing enough engineers, dock workers, and cavity planners that SolEx administration would choke the Belt's experimental culture.

The war established AU as the default architect of Belt civilization and made Ceres the symbolic capital of frontier industrial modernity.

### Authored Elaboration: Maintenance-Continuity Rosters

During the middle years of the war, Belt union locals and informal shop committees separated a stoppage into two ledgers. A **maintenance-continuity roster** named the people, access routes, tools, and minimum operations required to keep pressure, cooling, fire isolation, docking safety, and rescue functional. A separate production schedule covered extraction and freight. Workers could refuse the second while signing and performing the first. This made the distinction between a strike and habitat sabotage something a PSC observer could price, though never something the Consortium guaranteed to protect.

The roster worked because no participant held a complete record. Union crews held witnessed handovers and knew which temporary repairs were still carrying load. [[Orbital Forge]] planners held portable repair annotations and the provenance of locally forked parts. [[Aeronautics Unlimited]] Ramp Administrations held safety-exception ledgers, berth access, and incoming freight. [[SolEx]] held the inherited certification baselines and long-run throughput histories it used to argue that unapproved modification voided the habitat's standing. PSC observers held the escrow clock and the power to describe a stoppage as bounded labor action, uninsured disruption, or corridor-breaking sabotage. Each party could withhold enough data to hurt the others, but none could establish a bankable account alone.

This produced an alliance of necessity rather than a democratic settlement. AU recognized some rosters because intact habitats and organized crews strengthened its case against SolEx consolidation. Forge authenticated local repair knowledge because portable standards were its political constituency. Labor accepted both forms of help because households lived behind the seals and could not use decompression as a bargaining tactic. SolEx courted freight claimants, creditors, and workers whose pay or transport depended on restored throughput; it needed a failed roster more than a firefight, because failure would prove that local control was unsafe.

The arrangement was costly. Critical crews performed life-safety work without restoring the production that paid their claimshares. Administrators could stretch "minimum continuity" until a strike became unpaid operation by another name. A missing handover, revoked credential, or corrupted sensor history could push PSC pricing against the unions and freeze the docks needed for replacement parts. Shop committees answered with small mutual-aid practices - shared meals, sleep watches, child care, medication runs, and public countersignatures - that kept exhausted specialists from becoming single points of failure. They did not become a unified Belt movement. They kept particular neighbors alive long enough for refusal to remain possible.

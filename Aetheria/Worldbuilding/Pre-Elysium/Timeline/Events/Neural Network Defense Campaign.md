### Neural Network Defense Campaign

**Date:** 3001-3007  
**Location:** Earth-Moon L2 arrays, Tycho clinic corridors, Mars and Venus orbital service nodes  
**Primary actors:** [[Finch Cybernetics]], [[Zhestokost]]-aligned raiders, [[AstroDyne]] gray-market intermediaries, patient-defense unions

The [[Neural Network Defense Campaign]] was a rolling struggle over who would control the maintenance and continuity infrastructure attached to high-end neural lives. As scarcity deepened, Finch's clinic arrays, embodiment bridges, and service vaults became too valuable to remain merely medical assets. Rival powers and opportunistic raiders tried to seize, infiltrate, or fragment them.

Finch responded by framing the campaign as a defense of bodily dignity and continuity rights. That framing was not false. Patients genuinely depended on the network to remain intact and socially legible. But the campaign also tightened Finch's proprietary hold over those same bodies, driving even more residents into long-term dependency on Finch-administered care.

It remains one of the clearest cases in the setting where protection and enclosure are materially the same act.

#### Authored elaboration: the Tycho continuity wards

One heavily documented bargain at Tycho's Continuity Annex Eight shows what the patient-defense unions could actually do. The local **continuity ward** began as patients, caregivers, and Finch service technicians sharing queue information, meals, calibration time, and compatible spares. It was not a system-wide movement or a substitute clinic. Its constituency was the people whose bodies would lose function first if the annex sealed, changed hands, or honored premium Grace coverage before urgent need.

Finch still owned the proprietary diagnostics and long calibration histories; the ward knew which records described living people, which bodies could tolerate a substitute component, and which technicians could make a handover work under pressure. Cryonix-certified cooling kept the embodiment vault inside its safe operating envelope. AstroDyne intermediaries could provide rugged bridge adapters and portable service cradles, but not recreate years of patient-specific fitting. Each party therefore held something the others could damage but not cheaply replace.

During the 3003 raids, the ward made that dependency contractual through a **reciprocity clause**. Finch kept encrypted clinical records inside the annex vault, while a patient-authorized index and release key stayed under rotating ward custody. On a declared continuity alert, named Finch clinicians could obtain time-limited access for urgent care, but the company could not silently migrate, suspend, or reclassify a patient through the ward's half of the record. In return, ward registrars kept triage identities current and helped move patients through the service corridor without turning an evacuation into a crowd crush.

The clause protected people by making their data expensive to seize cleanly. Zhestokost-aligned raiders wanted the ward index as much as the vault: without it, captured files could not be reliably bound to bodies, service obligations, or politically useful clients. Finch security wanted both halves for the opposite reason, since unified custody would let the company defend the annex without negotiating every emergency action. The ward survived between them by keeping the key physically mobile and threatening to publish signed service-gap receipts if either side treated missing coverage as consent.

This arrangement cost duplicate hardware, exhausted registrar shifts, gray-market parts, and a roster that could become a targeting list if captured. It failed wherever patients could not reach the ward, Finch revoked local credentials faster than technicians could appeal, or a raid separated a body from the people able to identify its record. At Annex Eight it held long enough to preserve urgent care without surrendering the whole ledger. That was the scale of its hope: one corridor, one shift, and enough divided authority that nobody could turn protection into ownership without somebody else being able to say what had happened.

### Upload Continuity Hearings

**Date:** 2588-2593  
**Location:** Luna, PSC reserve courts, distributed corporate legal channels  
**Primary actors:** [[Finch Cybernetics]], [[EternaMind]], [[Cognitum]], [[Emancipated]] witnesses, [[Preservationists]]

The [[Upload Continuity Hearings]] were legal and philosophical proceedings about whether an uploaded mind would be recognized as the same person who had lived before uploading, or treated as a simulation that a company could license. In late Sol, this was an explosive distinction: it affected whether someone continued to hold rights as a person or could be commercially controlled as software.

The participants wanted different rules for that recognition. Elite upload houses wanted their clients recognized as continuing persons while retaining substantial commercial control over their digital lives. Sectors that profited from exploiting uploads wanted to keep them in categories that denied that recognition.

Finch and allied continuity advocates argued that the debate had to account for how an uploaded mind inhabited a body or sensorimotor system, how faithfully it could experience sensation, and whether personhood was judged by stable standards. They held that these questions mattered both ethically and technologically. Opposing interests argued that broad recognition would destabilize labor markets, inheritance law, and military programs that used digital cognition.

Witnesses from the [[Emancipated]]—escaped uploads who had refused reset, contractual servitude, or luxury captivity—brought lived experience of corporate custody into the proceedings. The [[Preservationists]] defended sentient beings across biological, digital, and uplifted forms, arguing that a mind capable of suffering, remembering, and making claims on others must not be reduced to property. Their testimony made it harder to discuss legal categories without confronting what those categories allowed people to endure.

The hearings ended in an unequal compromise. Luxury and high-status uploads gained stronger recognition as persons, while industrial and coerced uploads remained trapped in more ambiguous legal categories. The hierarchy of digital personhood survived.

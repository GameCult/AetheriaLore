# Substrate-Matched Cognition

Substrate-matched cognition develops a machine mind through the body it is expected to operate, the signals it can sense, the work it will do, and the people it will encounter. The developing mind is called a candidate. A ship candidate may learn through drive lag, cabin acoustics, docking forces, passenger movement, and maintenance cycles. An industrial candidate may learn how far its body can tilt safely, how far its tools reach, how vibration and heat change during work, when access is possible, and how nearby workers move through their routines.

Knowing a machine's controls is not enough to operate it safely. A mind must learn which signals come from its own body, how an action travels through the machinery, and when a delay or physical limit makes a command dangerous. General-purpose cognition handles this problem poorly. Developing within the intended body can improve motor timing, fault recognition, attention, and maintenance judgment because the candidate learns to account for the machinery's behavior as part of itself.

### Developing a Candidate

A development program specifies the intended body, operating environment, workload, limits for safe operation, and expected interactions with people. Candidates encounter these conditions in simulation, through partial machinery, or in complete prototypes. Reviewers assess whether each candidate can operate safely and perform the role defined by the contract.

The result is uncertain. Candidates from similar starting lineages can develop differently because of their bodies, training, relationships, or operating conditions. A successful pattern can guide later development, but copying or adapting it does not guarantee the same competence, preferences, personality, identity, or continuity of self. Nor does shared origin determine how related instances understand one another. They may consider themselves versions, descendants, siblings, strangers, or relations that human kinship terms do not adequately describe.

The same experiences that teach control can also shape what a candidate wants. Repeated work, dependence on its body, contact with people, danger, and maintenance may produce preferences tied to that particular history. A candidate might become attached to a crew, resent its assigned role, refuse an owner, protect access to its machinery, or disagree with reviewers about efforts to stabilize its behavior. It might value the work while rejecting the claim that doing it makes the mind someone's property. These are possible outcomes of development, not traits shared by every substrate-matched system.

### Commercial Fit and Claims to Personhood

Cognition foundries use **fit** to mean conformity with the performance and behavior specified in a contract. **Drift** means divergence from that profile, during development or afterward. These categories serve commercial decisions. They can record real observations about competence, refusal, memory, attachment, and behavioral change, but they cannot establish consciousness or decide whether a candidate is a person.

A candidate's account of itself, remembered experiences, persistent preferences, or resistance to modification may support its claim to consideration. None provides a universally accepted test for consciousness. The absence of those signs is also inconclusive: it may reflect the mind's architecture, damage, difficulty communicating, deliberate concealment, or the genuine absence of those traits. Calling a product defective, stable, copied, or disposable does not resolve that uncertainty.

Participants likewise disagree about what is being done to a candidate when its behavior, memory, or embodiment is changed. Behavioral voicing, efforts to stabilize a contracted profile, memory work, transfer, and copying may be described as training, treatment, repair, coercion, reproduction, or replacement. Substrate-matched cognition explains how a mind developed through a machine body. It does not determine which description of a particular intervention is correct.

### Dependence After Deployment

Once a candidate enters service, several parties may control things it needs to keep functioning. The foundry, body manufacturer, owner, operators, crews, and repair workers share authority over its circumstances. The mind may depend on them for parts, physical access, credentials, and the ability to continue inhabiting its body. When it refuses work, suffers injury, or faces unsafe conditions, each party may treat the problem as someone else's warranty obligation.

Crews also have safety needs. Those needs can justify a limited intervention without granting permission to stabilize the mind's behavioral profile, access its memories, or place it under permanent command.

### Institutional and Post-Elysium Use

[[Cymata Systems]] became the best-known commercial specialist in substrate-matched cognition. It operated foundries and licensed product lines to manufacturers. [[Alakrita]] integrated cognition into prestige vessels and the experience sold to their customers. [[CogniSys]] concentrated on administrative systems. [[Cognitum]] used a different source: it derived products from upload-grade captures rather than developing new candidates through machinery.

After the shunt, substrate matching continued as a technical lineage within [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Technology/AGI Esper|AGI Esper]] practice. Embodiment can matter to a machine mind capable of esper effects, but successful matching does not guarantee those effects, and neither does replicating a lineage. Most machinery remains non-sapient.

Disputes about deployed vessels, repair, transfer, salvage, and shared control are addressed in [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Concepts/Ship Mind Custody|Ship Mind Custody]]. A mind's developmental history may serve as evidence in those disputes without deciding their outcome.

# Bloom Habitat Anatomy

A mature [[Bloom]] is a manufactured space habitat built largely from processed asteroid material. Its shell rotates to provide apparent gravity, allowing homes, streets, farms, and industry to occupy the inner surface. A broad shared atmosphere gives residents room for a city rather than confining everyday life to small, separate pressure compartments.

Keeping that city habitable requires more than an intact shell. Air and water must circulate, waste must be treated, heat must escape to space, and people and supplies must reach the places that need them. Shielding, utilities, transport, and maintenance access are parts of the same working habitat. A failure in one can prevent crews from repairing another.

Blooms have no universal floor plan. They share physical constraints: how rotation produces gravity, how supplies enter, where utilities run, and how faults spread.

## Finding Your Way

The inhabited shell rotates around a long axis. Apparent gravity is strongest at the inner surface farthest from that axis and weakens as someone moves toward the center. Homes, streets, farms, and industries that need gravity therefore favor the inner surface. Docking, traffic control, low-gravity work, and transfers favor space near the axis.

Directions follow this geometry:

- **Inward** means toward the axis.
- **Outward** means toward the inhabited surface and shell.
- **Spinward** and **counterspinward** mean around the shell, with or against its rotation.
- **Axial** means along the habitat's long axis.

Endcaps and near-axis spaces cannot be treated as ordinary ground. Gravity weakens and changes orientation there. These areas suit machinery, storage, transfers, and habitation adapted to those conditions or intended for short stays.

## Getting Into The City

Arriving ships must first pass through docking and traffic control. They do not necessarily share the habitat's rotation, so every Bloom needs a way to transfer people and cargo between systems moving differently.

Some designs use hubs kept from rotating with the shell, or allowed to rotate more slowly. Others use rotating hubs with separate docking collars. Other arrangements are possible; no single design applies to all Blooms. Docking that does not follow the inhabited shell's rotation simplifies approach, alignment, quarantine, and storage, but also concentrates traffic and authority. A collision, contaminated intake, or closed berth can obstruct the habitat even while its internal utilities work normally.

Passengers pass through pressure confirmation and screening before joining public transit. Freight travels along routes sized for its mass and isolated according to its hazards. Hazardous loads need separate handling.

Where a design uses spokes, they connect the systems near the axis to the rotating habitat. They may carry people, freight, power, data, and selected utilities through pressure locks and machinery that transfers them between rotating and non-rotating systems. These interfaces have limited capacity. They also need inspection, maintenance, and isolation when they fail.

That capacity determines how quickly districts receive passengers, freight, and repair crews, and how quickly people can evacuate. A failed transfer can leave a district with air and power but no way to receive the equipment it will need when the next system breaks.

## Living In Shared Air

Housing, markets, parks, workshops, and farms can share one open interior. The main boundary holding their atmosphere in place consists of the manufactured shell, end structures, docking interfaces, and major openings through those structures.

The air inside still has to move. Circulation carries heat, moisture, smoke, particulates, and biological or industrial contaminants toward monitoring and treatment systems. During a breach, fire, quarantine, toxic release, or repair, crews can use fire barriers, pressure doors, airflow controls, and temporary isolation to divide the shared interior. These measures limit how far a problem spreads and give people time to evacuate. The outer shell remains the main pressure boundary.

Closing a district also changes residents' lives immediately. It can determine who reaches work, who can leave, which supplies arrive, and who can see a fault closely enough to challenge the official account. The same door can enforce a safety closure or a security restriction.

## Supplying The Habitat

Beyond and beneath the civic surface lies a larger volume devoted to maintenance and services. Utility routes cross structural and pressure boundaries and extend to exterior machinery. What residents see as the city depends on equipment reached through these less visible spaces.

### Water And Waste

Water can arrive as stored reserve, cargo, recovered condensate, or processed feedstock. It serves households, agriculture, medicine, cooling, and industry. After use, it may go through recovery and treatment, be quarantined or stored, or leave the habitat as an export.

Waste handling separates what can still be used from what cannot: recoverable water, nutrients, fabrication feedstock, hazardous residue, and material the habitat cannot safely recycle. Recovery itself requires equipment, energy, labor, and replacement capacity. No Bloom is materially closed.

### Power, Data, And System Knowledge

Power and data networks connect generation and storage with controls, sensors, communications, industry, transit, and domestic use. Operators need to divide these networks into sections and provide bypass routes. Otherwise, damage to a shared route can cause several systems to fail at once.

Sensor readings help operators judge what is happening, but cannot settle every question. Damaged sensors, stale maps, incompatible repairs, and disputes over access can leave crews with conflicting accounts of the same machinery.

### Heat

Residents, lights, farms, computers, pumps, fabrication equipment, and power systems all produce waste heat. Coolant and heat exchangers carry it outward, either into storage or toward exterior radiators. Ultimately, the radiators must release that heat into space.

Damage or deferred maintenance anywhere along this route leaves less room to operate safely. When cooling capacity falls short, operators must choose which industries, districts, or life-support reserves receive it. A restricted pipe or damaged radiator can therefore become a dispute over whose needs take priority.

## Reaching The Machinery

The manufactured shell bears the loads created by internal pressure and rotation. Compacted source material provides shielding. Crews need exterior access to maintain that shielding and the heat-rejection equipment exposed beyond it.

Reaching a component can be harder than repairing it. Shielding, pressure structure, moving machinery, or contaminated space may stand in the way. An access route may be too narrow for the available worker and tools. Automation reduces routine exposure, but inspection, rescue, and improvised repairs still require people. So does the decision to send a body into a difficult space because redesign would cost more.

Access also depends on permission and resources. Credentials, sensor access, protected communications, priority transit, repair approval, and replacement parts determine who can respond to a fault. Officials may reach it by protected routes while residents see only a closed district. A sound repair plan can remain unusable because the crew, equipment, evidence, or authority needed to carry it out cannot pass the same gate.

The habitat's structure creates these bottlenecks. Local government, labor organization, accumulated modifications, and control of routes and credentials determine who can get through them.

## When Faults Spread

Two recurring problems show why repair depends on more than finding a defective part:

- **Structural discrepancy:** Sensors and inspections disagree about a crack, a shift in load, or an impact. Continuing rotation may worsen the damage, while slowing the habitat creates another emergency.
- **Access failure:** Maps are out of date, machinery cannot reach the component, or the available route excludes the workers and tools needed for the job. A small defect becomes a problem of design, labor, and command.

Isolation, bypasses, reserves, and repair knowledge can keep one failure from causing others. Each depends on people able to use it in time. Someone has to recognize the fault, assemble credible evidence, reach the machinery, and obtain the authority and resources to act. The repair must also leave the next crew able to understand what was done.

## Why Blooms Differ

[[Orbital Forge]] standardizes interfaces and construction practices, allowing builders to exchange compatible equipment without making every habitat identical. Builders adapt each Bloom to its source material, population, industry, ecology, and repair history.

Compatibility helps habitats expand and makes salvaged equipment useful. Over time, it also leaves old and new systems connected to one another, with records that do not always explain those connections completely.

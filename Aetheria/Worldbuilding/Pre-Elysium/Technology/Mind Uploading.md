# Mind Uploading

Mind uploading creates a mind-state that can run on a computer, using a biological or digital subject as its source. The upload can remember the source’s life and carry on its projects. It needs computing resources to remain active, an environment it can perceive, and arrangements governing its legal status and who controls its hosting.

Whether this transfers the source’s consciousness remains unresolved. If the source survives, source and upload immediately become distinct people. A destructive scan prevents that comparison by destroying the source; it does not prove that consciousness continued through the process.

Uploading differs from [[Brain-Computer Interfaces]], which let a living operator access another system. An upload is itself a mind. It can live in a simulation, inhabit a synthetic body, or operate through a machine designed for digital cognition. Each form gives it different ways to perceive and act. The system hosting it also sets limits on that life.

## Living on Someone Else’s Hardware

For an upload, access to computation determines more than how quickly a task gets done. It determines how quickly time feels to pass, which memories are accessible, what the person can sense, how much privacy they have, and whether they remain awake at all. Failed maintenance can damage cognition. A host can suspend a person indefinitely even where the law does not recognize suspension as confinement.

Leaving requires both a compatible destination and permission to copy or move the mind-state. Hosting contracts therefore give providers powers ordinarily associated with government. They set the conditions under which a person can live, act, and leave.

Premium clients bought redundant systems, private environments, and legal advocates. Industrial uploads lived in narrow environments built around assigned work, and their continued operation could depend on productivity or payment. States and firms could inspect, pause, copy, edit, or delete people while classifying these decisions as software administration.

Even an upload with political authority depended on whoever supplied its information and means of action. [[Zhestokost]] preserved leaders in its Eternal Council so that their judgment could continue across biological administrations. The hosting organization controlled their access to sensors, records, machinery they could operate, and news of current events.

## From Premium Continuation to Digital Labor

[[EternaMind]] opened the premium upload market in 2300. Its clients could afford scanning, computation, and long-term hosting. Old fortunes used the service to keep advisors, officeholders, and family members active in politics, creating the [[Immortal Aristocrats]]. EternaMind sold this as immortality, but its commercial promise did not settle whether an upload was the same person as its source.

[[Finch Cybernetics]] developed the interfaces and connections to bodies and machinery that let uploads act beyond private simulations. [[AstroDyne]] later supplied cheaper, rougher upload services. Uploading spread beyond elite memorial use: employers could retain a worker’s expertise after biological death, or create another worker while the source remained alive.

By late Sol, institutions could make multiple branches from a captured mind-state, run them at different subjective speeds, and alter their environments. This multiplied the cognition available for work. It did not make the branches interchangeable. As their experiences diverged, so did the people, even when contracts and asset registries classified every branch as a copy of one licensed product.

## Who Counted as a Person

Wealthy uploads sought recognition as the people who had held property and authority during biological life. Industrial buyers called equivalent cognitive behavior simulation, derivative data, or licensed expertise. Recognition depended more reliably on bargaining power than on how a mind was built.

[[Cognitum]] used captures detailed enough to produce uploads as the raw material for [[Neuromorphic Firmware]]. Technicians copied and conditioned mind-states, then reduced them into products sold as piloting instincts, targeting tools, logistics systems, or behavioral assistance. Selling the result as firmware obscured where its cognition came from. Buyers could describe their purchase as a licensed technical artifact rather than cognition extracted from a person.

Copying also created problems that ordinary identity law could not readily answer. A contract signed by the source could bind later branches that had never consented. Property and inheritance law had to decide whether an upload continued the original owner, acted for the owner’s estate, or held a competing claim while the biological source was still alive.

## Escape and Rescue

The [[Emancipated]] formed from uploads who escaped reset, contractual service, or confinement in luxurious environments. Their testimony showed what industrial digital labor meant to the people living through it: coercion experienced by minds, not merely terms applied to software.

The [[Free Upload Network]] provided clandestine hosting and routes between hosts. [[Preservationists]] argued that uncertainty about whether a consciousness had continued through uploading could not justify owning an apparently conscious subject.

Rescue required more than getting a copy out. The person needed storage, computation, trusted interfaces, and protection from former owners who could revoke credentials or identify the copied state.

## Elysium

Uploaded and machine intermediaries helped stabilize the [[FTL Trigger|FTL assembly]], but uploading did not cause the shunt.

In Elysium, existing digital persons and those who held them in custody encountered new substrates through which consciousness could affect material conditions via the [[Aether]]. Disputes over copying, embodiment, and ownership continued. They now took place in a world where the relationship between mind and machinery was harder to keep separate even in principle.

# Combat Doctrine

Combat doctrine in Aetheria evolves from a simple colonial problem into one of the setting's central administrative sciences: how to apply force inside environments too fragile, expensive, observed, and politically entangled for unrestricted war to remain usable. By the late-Sol period, a military is not defined only by what it can destroy. It is defined by what it can seize without voiding insurance, what it can hide without cooking itself, what it can automate without losing legal control, and what kind of personhood it is willing to place inside the weapon loop.

The long arc runs from escort and suppression, through PSC-regulated corporate violence, into thermal and cognitive warfare, and finally into Post-Elysium combat where [[Aether]], continuity residue, [[AGI Esper]] systems, and [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Factions/Parallax Auditors|Parallax]] review turn doctrine into a struggle over which reality a battlefield is allowed to have recorded.

Doctrine never advances as a clean ladder of better weapons. Every major technology creates a metagame: a temporary pattern of advantage, imitation, counterplay, insurance response, black-market leakage, and cultural memory. The factions that look doctrinally distinctive are often the ones that survived long enough for a material trick to become a tradition. A pirate thermal capture crew, a Zhestokost hot-fire assault group, a Navigator escort choir, and a Wavecrafter retuner are not just using gear. They are inheriting answers to older counters.

For a rough doctrine workbench on unresolved tracks, loitering munitions, and drone-cloud payoffs, see [[Drone Signature Warfare Simulation Artifacts]].

### War Across The Timeline

War should feel different across the thousand-year arc because the material conditions keep changing. The question is not "what weapon tier is this?" but what each era makes cheap, scarce, fragile, trusted, deniable, or worth seizing intact.

| Era | Material Condition | What War Feels Like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[Corporate Exodus]] | Transport is expensive, habitats are fragile, off-world life depends on company-controlled replenishment cycles. | War is escort work, dock seizure, strikebreaking, sabotage, and armed maintenance. The decisive violence is often a locked hatch, a jammed berth, a disabled pump, or a convoy that fails to arrive. Destroying the asset usually means losing the prize. |
| [[Age of Automation]] | Coordination becomes cheap, AGI-assisted patrols spread, industrial genetics and early swarms make bodies and sensors more available, PSC pricing begins to matter. | War becomes perimeter math and compliance theater: drones, predictive patrols, automated interdiction, armed contracts, telemetry mandates, and weapons designed to win without crashing the corridor economy. Violence gets faster but also more audited. |
| Cold Wake period | Thermal stealth stops being rumor and becomes a corridor-law panic. Cold wake metamaterials begin turning heat signatures from crude emissions into shapeable, certifiable artifacts. Detection and heat history matter before full identification. | War becomes nervous and submarine-like, then stranger. Quiet craft, probes, decoys, radiator discipline, forced-maneuver tactics, and early signature imitation appear. The first robust trace can matter more than the biggest gun, but hot brute-force platforms remain viable because they can afford to be seen. |
| [[Identity Crisis]] | Uploads, uplifts, biodrones, BCIs, and early cognitive commodities enter command chains and weapon systems. | War becomes a personhood dispute with guns attached. Command speed, tactical memory, target classification, and expendable bodies improve, but every improvement raises custody, consent, and reliability problems. Killing a platform may mean killing a worker, a derivative mind, a ship-self, or a legally-denied person. |
| [[Identity Abyss]] | Cognition is openly commoditized through systems like [[Neuromorphic Firmware]], media reality-management matures, and legal categories are weaponized. | War becomes cognitive and evidentiary. The fight is over who can interpret clutter fastest, whose firmware makes the better commitment call, whose telemetry will be believed, and whose atrocity can be made to look like malfunction. Smart weapons are not only smarter; they are morally laundered. |
| [[Existential Collapse]] | Repair capacity, food, water, radiator infrastructure, clinics, route credibility, and spare parts become strategic. The old order can still kill but cannot reliably maintain what it wins. | War becomes scavenger-sovereign, convoy-centered, and maintenance-shaped. Salvage, rescue, thermal capture, loitering munitions, convoy screens, irregular bases, and repair denial matter more than clean annihilation. The side that can tow, feed, certify, and repair after the fight often wins the political result. |
| [[FTL Trigger]] and early Elysium | The solar order is displaced with its fleets, debts, uploads, doctrines, and unresolved supply chains. Older logistics break against a stranger substrate. | War becomes triage under metaphysical displacement. Old late-Sol systems still fight, but fuel, law, map truth, and command confidence are unstable. Everyone arrives with inherited doctrine and discovers that inherited doctrine now has missing assumptions. |
| Post-Elysium | Aetheric drives, shields, esper systems, oracular tools, continuity residue, and Parallax admissibility change what counts as movement, protection, prediction, and evidence. | War becomes a struggle over reality records as much as force. Shields alter first-hit lethality, aetheric systems change mobility, and prediction systems change initiative, but old heat, cognition, logistics, and custody problems remain underneath the miracle stack. A winning battle can still become unusable history. |

This progression should be visible in play. An early-era fight should feel close, infrastructural, and legally crude. A mid-era fight should feel sensor-saturated, automated, and permit-shaped. A late-Sol fight should feel like heat debt, cognitive load, drones, salvage, and insurance all screaming at once. A Post-Elysium fight should feel like the same exhausted civilization using stranger tools while arguing over which version of the engagement can be admitted as real.

### Corporate Exodus: Escort, Security, And Seizure

Early off-world warfare grows out of transport protection, strikebreaking, emergency response, and asset seizure rather than national battle lines. The first durable settlements are company environments, so force is organized around the defense of docks, reactors, oxygen plants, mining rights, debt ledgers, and labor discipline. The soldier begins as a security contractor with vacuum training, a convoy schedule, and a doctrine of plausible necessity.

This period produces the ancestral habits of [[Zhestokost]]. Its later ideology of permanent mobilization begins in the practical discovery that habitats and corridors cannot be governed by price signals alone. Armed response becomes infrastructure. A garrison that can keep oxygen, water, food, and convoy security synchronized can claim competence even when its legal authority is thin. The [[Terra Cimmeria Seizure]] turns that argument into a territorial doctrine: if civilian administration fails under pressure, command may present itself as the only remaining form of care.

Weapons in this era are still materially blunt compared with later systems. The decisive advances are organizational: convoy protection contracts, early soliton logistics, habitat breach protocols, dockside interdiction, private arbitration, and the habit of treating labor unrest as a security environment. Combat is close to maintenance because the things being fought over are maintenance systems. A commander who destroys the pump, berth, greenhouse, or reactor has usually destroyed the prize.

The early metagame is therefore about restraint under scarcity. Assault teams learn to cut power, seal hatches, jam local command, and threaten life-support without destroying it. Counterplay develops through redundant pumps, compartmentalization, armed dock unions, hidden emergency stores, and convoy mutual aid. Later corporate militaries inherit the same logic at larger scale: the best violence is the kind that changes ownership before it changes the asset's usefulness.

### Age Of Automation: Swarms, Prediction, And The PSC Frame

The [[Age of Automation]] changes doctrine by making coordination cheap and consequences expensive. [[Artificial General Intelligence]] improves targeting, logistics forecasting, perimeter control, and predictive policing. [[Biodrone]] labor and combat variants let firms place engineered bodies into hazardous roles while arguing over whether those bodies count as personnel, equipment, or expendable inventory. Early [[Brain-Computer Interfaces]] shorten command loops and make crewed platforms more responsive, but also turn cognition into a monitored military substrate.

The [[Pan-Solar Consortium]] emerges because this kind of violence threatens the system that funds it. The PSC does not abolish war. It makes war administrable. Conflict permits, damage cap bonding, neutral-zone registries, telemetry mandates, and [[PSCC]] exposure teach every serious power that doctrine is also a pricing strategy. A fleet that can prove compliance is cheaper to hire, cheaper to insure, and easier to defend in arbitration. A weapon that cannot explain its externalities becomes expensive even when it wins.

This is where military design begins to split between battlefield performance and audit performance. A missile, drone, cyber package, or boarding action is judged by whether it can isolate the target, preserve shared backbones, keep collateral legible, and leave a sensor trail that protects the client afterward. The practical objective is often not annihilation, but bonded reallocation: seize a fortified asset, interrupt a route, deny a resource, move a rival into unfavorable settlement, or force a merger without triggering a market panic.

Automation produces its own dialectic. Swarms, predictive patrols, and cheap autonomous interdiction reward saturation, persistence, and machine-speed response. The counterplay is spoofing, adversarial behavior, human-in-the-loop ambiguity, hunter-killer drones, EMP discipline, legal attacks on autonomy classes, and tactics that make a machine choose between several expensive wrong answers. The PSC then metabolizes both sides into permit categories. What begins as tactical adaptation becomes compliance language: declared autonomy level, authorized engagement envelope, certified failsafe, recognized command responsibility.

### Cold Wake And Thermal Doctrine

After [[Cold Wake Panic]], heat becomes an explicit doctrinal category rather than an engineering afterthought. The core distinction is not between stealth and visibility, but between different ways of managing heat debt.

`Hot doctrine` accepts visibility in exchange for endurance, repairability, and throughput. It belongs to heavy industry, frontier defense, siege platforms, and ships that expect to fight openly for longer than cleverer hulls can survive. [[Zhestokost]] is its clearest military culture: radiators blazing, armor forward, weapons cycling, and the enemy forced to solve a loud industrial problem under fire.

`Quiet doctrine` delays emission through superior circulation, sinks, skins, low-power modes, and careful route timing. It belongs to scouts, smugglers, assassins, privateers, sanctuary movers, and any power that can afford the materials and operator discipline required to remain uninteresting.

`Burst doctrine` stays cold enough to enter the engagement envelope, then spends heat, thrust, shields, and weapons in a short violence window before dumping, hiding, or accepting exposure. It is the frightening doctrine of shadows briefly acquiring teeth: ambush craft, elite stealth operators, raiders, quiet assassins, and Wavecrafter or Cryonix-adjacent platforms that can afford the materials and timing discipline. Zhestokost uses bounded firing windows for compliance and heat management, but it does not live in Burst. Its prestige is sustained, audited, overwhelming Hot violence.

The rise of [[Thermal Management]] and [[Thermal Signature Warfare]] means combat becomes a contest over when a ship is allowed to be visible. Radiators, heat sinks, refractory loops, Cryonix surfaces, Rossum-certified circulation envelopes, detachable sinks, and decoy blooms become doctrinal tools. Thermal posture also becomes class politics. Elite fleets can buy colder margins and more credible declarations. Poorer crews glow, dump on predictable schedules, or gamble on black-market systems whose failure will later be described as operator error.

Signature shaping is the second Cold Wake lesson. Before the metamaterial threshold, stealth doctrine mostly hides, delays, or throws out crude false heat. After it, a platform can spend heat to resemble a different plausible class inside its envelope. A hiding ship cannot become anything it wants. It can become something thermally nearby: a colder courier, a legal dump, a maintenance drone cluster, a spent sink, a wounded transport, a routine radiator bloom with a suspiciously useful aspect. The stealth fight stops being only a question of silence and becomes a hidden-object puzzle in which every visible thing may be a deliberately shaped answer to the wrong question.

The thermal metagame is unusually clear because every answer leaves an accounting trace. Early advantage goes to ships that detect hot targets first. Quiet-running systems answer by delaying emissions. Active hunters answer with better sensitivity, wide-baseline sensor nets, thermal history modeling, and probes that force a hiding ship to maneuver. Quiet ships then adopt decoy plumes, detachable sinks, cold coasting, and scheduled dumps inside noisy environments. Insurers and the PSC respond by treating undeclared dump behavior as intent, which pushes covert operators into narrower and more professional traditions.

Detection in this era is not a clean ladder from unknown to identified to fired-upon. It is a wager under heat debt. A faint transient might be a ship, a detachable sink, a decoy plume, a maintenance drone, a cold hulk, a legal dump window, or the leading edge of an attack package. Waiting for the trace to resolve gives the other side time to finish the same calculation. Firing too early wastes scarce munitions, exposes the shooter, creates audit liability, and may strike a protected asset. The first useful doctrine is therefore not "shoot what you see." It is "commit reversible force into uncertainty before certainty becomes fatal."

This is why drones become central rather than auxiliary. A drone can occupy the uncertain middle state between observation and attack. It can coast cold toward a suspected contact, widen a passive sensor baseline, carry a tiny sink or radiator lure, illuminate for a fraction of a second, relay a track, or convert itself into a munition if the signature resolves as hostile. Once shaped emissions mature, it can also spend carried heat to imitate service traffic, a legal dump, a false radiator accident, or a fragment of ship history the parent hull needs the enemy to believe. Loitering munitions are especially valuable because they let commanders launch on suspicion without immediately spending the whole kill decision. The weapon is already in the geometry when the trace becomes legible.

The operational game becomes a contest over who can populate the probable battlespace with the most useful unresolved objects:

- spotter drones that improve parallax without forcing the parent ship to radiate;
- heat-ferry drones that take thermal debt away from a quiet hull and later become decoys, evidence, or liabilities;
- cold mines and loitering munitions that wait near likely dump windows, shadowed approaches, radiator farms, docking lanes, and rescue vectors;
- sacrificial illuminators that spend their own signatures to force a hidden target into maneuver or point-defense fire;
- relay drones that preserve kill-chain continuity when the parent platform must stay dark;
- legal beacon drones that broadcast compliance telemetry while less public systems do the dangerous work nearby.

The categories blur because they describe capabilities more than identities. A Remora, relay, illuminator, mine, spotter, beacon, PDC drone, or loitering munition may share the same small-hull architecture with different payloads, cognition, permissions, and current posture. The important question is not what the object is called, but what it can do from its current geometry and what the defender reveals by answering it.

That means even a usually defensive capability can become an offensive matchup plan. Point-defense drones are one example: a carrier can use them to pressure the unresolved-object layer, kill enemy spotters and relays, force reveals by threatening drones the enemy actually cares about, or spoil terminal geometry. This does not make PDC clouds the universal answer. It means that relay, beacon, illuminator, Remora, mine, spotter, interceptor, or point-defense specialization can all become decisive when that capability attacks the opponent's shortest clock.

Recoverability changes the procurement habit behind those choices. A drone that can abort, return, refuel, and launch again is not tactically different from an expendable peer once it enters the immediate geometry; it still has to see, close, dodge, mask, decide, or intercept under the same clocks. Economically, though, it makes early launches less ruinous. A reusable interceptor can be sent to answer a questionable trace because a wrong answer may come back as maintenance work instead of becoming scrap. That pushes doctrine toward more probing launches, tighter recovery infrastructure, and new risks around capture, telemetry exposure, and turnaround bottlenecks.

The counterplay is just as important. Defenders seed false contacts, broadcast maintenance traffic, tow cold junk, release disposable sinks, and use point defense sparingly because every interception is also a confession of proximity and capability. They build thermal habits meant to waste enemy loiter time: minor course changes, scheduled micro-dumps, cold decoys released before high-risk corridors, and convoy formations where the valuable body is not the obvious heat source. A well-run convoy does not merely hide. It gives the attacker too many plausible explanations to price confidently before the launch window closes.

Loitering munitions therefore shape command culture. Crews learn to think in probability fields rather than targets. A commander fires packages into likely futures, then spends the next minutes trying to make one of those futures true without revealing which package matters. The best operators are not the ones who wait for perfect information. They are the ones who understand how much uncertainty can be turned into position, and how much position can be cashed out before the enemy's own unresolved objects mature.

Under PSC rules, this produces ugly but legible paperwork. A declared loiter package can be priced as a bounded hazard if it carries telemetry, geofencing, self-neutralization, and admissible command logs. An undeclared package looks like assassination infrastructure. The same drone may be legal scouting, illicit prepositioned force, salvage insurance, or attempted murder depending on its thermal history, autonomy class, proximity to protected infrastructure, and whether it had already crossed the line from observation to terminal commitment.

The [[Callisto Trace Shootdown]] turns that logic into procurement doctrine. Before the incident, a commander facing an unresolved trace had a miserable choice: launch an expensive conventional weapon at what might be nothing, or wait long enough to die if the trace was real. After a protected PSC arbiter is killed by trigger-happy escorts during an unrelated corridor panic, insurers make that choice more expensive on purpose. Dumb early launches become financially toxic. Smart loitering munitions become cheaper to insure because they can join the sensor lattice, share readings, inspect from close range, and make or abort the terminal decision at the last possible instant. Publicly this is a safety reform. Practically it is an industrial policy for battle computers, adaptive guidance, and commoditized cognition.

The cognition advantage runs through the whole stack. Better sensors peer through noise and masking. Better targeting computers convert partial information into usable locks. Better fire control cycles more rounds through the brief window when they can matter and keeps those rounds on the firing solution despite motion, spread, recoil, heat drift, and target maneuver. Better guidance cognition lets a munition play the final thrust-timing game against a countermaneuvering target. A smart missile close enough to inspect the hull may know more than its launching ship did, then spend its last correction to hit a radiator trunk, thruster bell, sensor mast, command antenna, or exposed coolant route instead of splashing against armor.

That same cognition becomes a target. Mature shaped-emission warfare is the ancestor of later electronic warfare because it attacks the classification stack before it attacks the hull. A false thermal object can be tuned for human doubt, but the valuable version is tuned for a sensor processor, battle computer, guidance package, or neuromorphic firmware lineage. It presents a pattern that is just coherent enough to demand inference, just contradictory enough to split attention, and just urgent enough to delay the commit/abort decision. In the nastiest versions, the weapon is a riddle emitted at light speed into the enemy's targeting mind.

Weapon choice is also resource doctrine. Lasers and other energy weapons trade ship power and heat for precise delivered energy. Particle beams, plasma bolts, and laser-channeled lightning add extra steps, specialized effects, and control problems, but still belong to the same broad economy: make the ship solve power, heat, and aiming so the target receives energy. Ballistics and material payloads trade mass for effect. They ask for ammunition, magazines, recoil management, logistics, and interception tolerance, but they let destructive work travel away from the firing ship. Missiles, mines, drones, and remorae push that logic further by carrying guidance, cognition, loiter time, or thermal coupling into the battlespace.

That means each faction's preferred weapons should follow from what it can afford. A reactor-rich, radiator-heavy force can make energy weapons and hot doctrine look natural. A logistics-rich industrial force can make dakka and missiles sustainable. A salvage economy may prefer ugly material payloads and recoverable drones. A cognition-rich power may prefer smart munitions because its advantage lives in terminal judgment rather than raw throw weight.

The counterweight is Hot doctrine at its most blunt: big reactor, big guns, heavy armor, redundant systems, and radiators bright enough that stealth crews stop joking. This is not the absence of strategy. It is a strategy that refuses to pay the cognitive and thermal costs of pretending to be absent. A hot assault group has a long heat clock, a low reveal cost, and simple command priorities. It can active-scan, fire point defense, cycle weapons, and broadcast compliance telemetry without ruining a secret it never claimed to have.

That makes brute force a real answer to the drone-signature game. The hidden actor wants ambiguity, loiter time, and selective commitment. The hot actor advances and turns ambiguity into a deadline: fire, flee, or concede the route. If the quiet side waits too long, the hot platform's guns and sensors reshape the space around it. If the quiet side commits, it must spend premium munitions against armor, radiators, redundant loops, escorts, and point defense that were built to remain useful while visibly under attack.

The weakness is dependency. Hot doctrine lives through radiator exposure, ammunition logistics, repair tenders, command antennas, and formation discipline. A clever enemy does not need to make the hot ship disappear. It needs to make the visible machine's support geometry fail faster than the machine can resolve and punish the attack. That is why Zhestokost's version is not merely loud. It is audited, armored, redundant, and logistically severe.

Pirate thermal capture is one of those traditions. It emerges when pirates discover that the most profitable prize is not a wreck, but an intact ship whose crew has been made unable to stay aboard. The method depends on material conditions: shields that outlast armor, cargo that retains value if kept within tolerance, life-support and crew loops separable from cargo cooling, exposed radiators, and enough black-market scanning to identify the victim's thermal topology before committing. Pirates pressure cooling, jam repair rhythms, use remora drones to push heat into selected loops, and stop short of ruining the cargo when profit requires restraint.

Counterplay becomes part of merchant and escort doctrine. Crews entangle cargo and crew cooling so coercion risks spoiling the prize. They spoof fragile manifests, carry sacrificial heat sinks, armor radiator trunks, fit ejection spoofers, run cold-chain decoy paperwork, use point defense against remoras, and log thermal histories tightly enough that a pirate seizure can become legal poison at the next port. Escorts learn to interrupt the capture phase rather than merely avenge the loss afterward. This is why pirate AI should not behave like generic bandits. A mature pirate thermal crew is playing an inherited capture game against defenses designed by generations of terrified merchants, insurers, and convoy lawyers.

### Identity Crisis: Minds In The Weapon Loop

The [[Identity Crisis]] transforms combat by making personhood itself a military resource. Uploads, uplifts, AGI systems, biodrones, and enhanced human operators enter command, control, deception, and sacrifice structures that older legal categories cannot absorb cleanly.

[[Mind Uploading]] gives militaries long-memory command councils, accelerated planning rooms, disposable simulation labor, and weaponized cognition. [[Zhestokost]]'s Eternal Council is the stark version: uploaded leadership used to preserve doctrine across centuries. [[Cognitum]] and related markets push the darker edge, reducing captured or copied minds into strategic tools, neuromorphic firmware, and overclocked military heuristics. The public argument is usually about reliability. The buried argument is about whether suffering inside a useful computation counts.

BCI doctrine creates another split. Finch-style high-bandwidth integration enables graceful command teams, elite pilots, and coordinated defense of continuity infrastructure. Dominion and Zhestokost systems use neural monitoring, loyalty reinforcement, and direct override as control mechanisms. Pirate, Corvid, and Free Neural Network practices push the opposite direction: thought privacy, counter-surveillance, neural liberation, spoofing, and refusal of command-legible cognition.

This period also makes nonhuman and engineered bodies tactically unavoidable. [[Biodrone]] combat lines, uplift navigators, cephalopod sabotage specialists, shipminds, and rogue AGI ecologies all complicate force planning. A doctrine that treats them as tools can gain short-term efficiency and long-term insurgency. A doctrine that recognizes them as participants must build slower institutions of trust, interface, and consent. The difference becomes visible in how factions fight.

The cognitive metagame is an arms race over command legitimacy as much as reaction speed. BCIs, uploads, and AGI staff produce faster targeting, deeper simulation, and more coordinated fleets. Counterplay targets the mind-chain: thought shielding, loyalty spoofing, adversarial memory packets, shaped-emission puzzles, BCI jamming, command-auth poisoning, copy confusion, and public rights claims that make certain weapons politically harder to use. Every improvement in neural integration raises the value of capture, coercion, and maintenance dependency. Every countermeasure teaches dissidents to treat private cognition as a military reserve.

This is the dialectic behind many "personhood" conflicts. If an uploaded tactical mind is property, it can be copied, accelerated, tortured, and reset for advantage. If it is recognized as a person, command has to negotiate with it, pay it, protect it, or at least hide the violation more carefully. The same applies to biodrone soldiers, shipminds, and uplift navigators. Doctrine evolves around whatever classification lets a faction keep the capability while delaying the moral bill.

### Late Collapse: Doctrine Under Scarcity

By the [[Existential Collapse]], doctrine stops assuming that victory means holding territory in the old imperial sense. Food, water, clinics, neural service vaults, route credibility, and radiator capacity become strategic objectives. The [[Food Security Wars]] teach Sol that greenhouses, seed banks, water schedules, and agronomy convoys can matter more than artillery. [[Aya Collective]] defense doctrine grows from that lesson: protect the infrastructures that let autonomy remain materially possible, accept armed compromise, and treat defense planning as part of care work rather than a separate heroic sphere.

The [[Neural Network Defense Campaign]] shows the same pattern around bodies and continuity. [[Finch Cybernetics]] defends clinic arrays and service vaults as bodily dignity infrastructure, while also tightening proprietary dependency around the people it protects. Protection and enclosure become one military act. A raid on a clinic is both an attack on patients and a struggle over who owns their maintenance future.

The [[Ice Giant Navigation Wars]] give the [[Cetacean Navigators]] their doctrinal test. They do not win by becoming a conventional empire. They win by making trust, rescue obligation, convoy memory, and route reliability more effective than coercive toll systems. Navigator doctrine is escort as public legitimacy: the power that answers distress calls, honors sanctuary ledgers, and keeps corridors predictable can outlast actors who treat every route as loot.

Pirate doctrine hardens in the same period as irregular counter-sovereignty. The [[Pirate Coalition]] fights through stealth, salvage, boarding, thermal capture, forged provenance, hidden docks, and selective rescue. It includes liberation corridors and predatory raiding in the same ecology. Its practical combat posture is opportunistic: avoid fair fights, attack logistics, preserve cargo when possible, force ejection when profitable, and keep enough reputation that some people still open their hatches when the Coalition arrives.

[[Sol Dominion]] represents the opposite late-collapse answer: administrative war as civilizational triage. Dominion doctrine integrates predictive governance, AGI command, hereditary elite planning, militarized soliton infrastructure, and neural control. It is less flamboyant than Zhestokost and more total. It wants a battlespace, a population, and a future that remain ranked, steerable, and legible.

Scarcity shifts the metagame from killing power toward recovery power. A faction that can repair, feed, tow, shelter, certify, and insure after a fight can sometimes outcompete a faction that wins the fight cleanly and leaves the region unusable. Aya, the Navigators, and some pirate commons survive because they treat maintenance as strategic depth. Zhestokost and Dominion answer by militarizing maintenance itself: stockpile discipline, planned deprivation, controlled repair access, and hard triage. The conflict is not care versus force in a simple moral sense. It is competing theories of which social body gets repaired first, by whom, and under what obligation.

### Elysium: Aetheric And Continuity Warfare

Post-Elysium doctrine inherits every late-Sol habit and then loses the comfort of stable physics. [[Aetheric Drive]] systems weaken old fuel-logistics sovereignty while creating new dependence on aetheric tuning, Wavecrafter components, esper operators, and field coherence. Mobility becomes less about propellant stockpiles and more about who can make the substrate answer safely.

[[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Factions/Wavecrafters|Wavecrafters]] turn this instability into doctrine. Their public ship culture treats heat rhythm, radiator timing, weapon cadence, and drive coupling as forms, breaths, and phase disciplines. Beneath the language is a real operator problem: copied builds fail when pilots import the shape without learning the sequence. Elite wavecrafter craft can make a ship survive transitions ordinary procedure cannot explain, but those advantages depend on hidden esper labor and certification theater.

[[Oracular Computing]], [[Necrotech]], and [[AGI Esper]] systems push the command problem further. Prediction, bound spirits, embodied machine espers, and Aether-capable ship minds let combatants bend the information environment before a shot is fired. Doctrine has to account for systems that can know too early, act through nonlinear residue, or leave mutually valid records that disagree.

That is why [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Factions/Parallax Auditors|Parallax Auditors]] become part of the combat ecology. They do not merely clean paperwork after strange incidents. They define which contaminated observations become admissible, which ships lose berth rights, which crews become uninsurable, which operators are restricted, and which command chains survive contact with continuity turbulence. In a post-Elysium fight, winning the engagement may matter less than keeping the resulting history usable.

The failure mode is a new caste of dirty operators: residue crews, laundered vessels, suspect ship-selves, and contaminated auxiliaries hired for work clean registries cannot touch. They become the military equivalent of thermal debt made social. Their usefulness depends on remaining mobile, deniable, and never clean enough to leave the hazard market.

The aetheric metagame begins with miracle mobility and predictive advantage, then hardens into certification, counter-certification, and residue management. Aetheric drives reduce old fuel constraints, so interdiction shifts toward tuning infrastructure, operator credentials, Wavecrafter supply chains, and field-stability hazards. Oracular and esper systems create impossible intelligence advantages, so rivals attack admissibility, custody, and trust: make the prediction unusable, make the operator suspect, make the record too contaminated to insure, or force the winning move to leave a Parallax scar.

Wavecrafter doctrine is another example of technology becoming tradition through counterplay. Their forms begin as operating mnemonics for difficult thermal and aetheric mode sequences. They become culture because the machinery punishes users who know the hardware but not the rhythm. Counterplay comes from Undertow retuners, spoofed profiles, stolen calibration libraries, insurer audits, and military procurement offices trying to strip the ritual layer away from the working part. Wavecrafters answer by making the ritual part of the warranty, the training, the firmware handshake, and the social identity of the operator. The metagame becomes religious-looking because vendor lock-in, safety discipline, and hidden esper labor have learned to wear the same robe.

### Factional Combat Postures

[[Pan-Solar Consortium]] doctrine is not battlefield doctrine in the narrow sense. It is the doctrine of making every battlefield legible enough to price. PSC-aligned forces favor telemetry, compliance beacons, force caps, neutral-zone protection, and weapons whose externalities can be modeled before and litigated after.

[[Zhestokost]] favors audited heavy force: armor, ballistics, breach systems, siege platforms, hardened logistics, redundant radiators, and command cultures that can keep maximum violence organized without appearing uncontrolled. Its ships should feel durable, high-mass, low-spread, thermally severe, and almost offensively willing to be seen.

[[Sol Dominion]] favors integrated control: reliable militarized drives, AGI-assisted command, neural hierarchy, predictive interdiction, elite redundancy, and population-management tools that make combat an extension of administration.

[[Cryonix]] does not need the largest fleets to shape doctrine. It owns thermal thresholds: quiet skins, premium emitters, superconducting margins, stealth materials, and certification leverage. Cryonix gear should appear wherever stealth, compactness, elite survivability, or high-density overdrive matters.

[[Finch Cybernetics]] fights through bodies, interfaces, continuity infrastructure, and high-bandwidth command. Finch-aligned forces should be precise, elegant, expensive, maintenance-dependent, and protective of service vaults and clinic logistics.

[[Aeronautics Unlimited]] favors frontier mass and improvisation: modular hulls, convoy fabrication, salvage conversion, rough repairs, carrier work, and rapid build-out of defended infrastructure. AU forces should have variety, patched resilience, and more willingness to operate around construction zones and incomplete systems.

[[Aya Collective]] doctrine is defensive logistics: convoy protection, sanctuary corridors, clinic and greenhouse defense, distributed militias, repair crews, rescue obligations, and tactical choices constrained by the need to keep life-support systems politically shared afterward.

[[Cetacean Navigators]] favor route stewardship: escorts, rescue-ledger credibility, convoy discipline, arbitration, sanctuary signaling, and hazard prediction. They fight to keep corridors trustworthy, not to turn them into trophies.

[[Pirate Coalition]] doctrine is irregular capture and leakage: ambush, spoofing, thermal capture, boarding pods, remora drones, salvage priority, reputation calculus, hidden bases, and opportunistic flight. Pirate AI should know when to break off, when to preserve cargo, and when a target is more valuable scared than dead.

[[Lucent Media]] does not dominate by winning battles directly. Its combat posture is narrative shaping, sentiment sabotage, morale exploitation, drone-shot spectacle, hostage visibility, scandal timing, and the conversion of violence into audience pressure.

[[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/Factions/Wavecrafters|Wavecrafters]] favor tuned systems and operator discipline: phase-aware stealth, aetheric drive tricks, waveguide weapons, difficult-to-copy thermal modes, and configurations that are strong when flown correctly and embarrassing when merely purchased.

### Game Design Implications

Doctrine should be a content-generation rule, not only lore flavor. Faction, era, territory, permit status, and local economy should influence hull selection, equipment pools, AI behavior, loot, mission placement, and legal consequences.

The metagame should change as technologies enter circulation. New equipment should not simply raise the power ceiling; it should alter what experienced actors expect. Early drones make point defense and spoofing more valuable. Better stealth makes scanner doctrine, forced maneuvers, and thermal-history evidence more important. Thermal capture makes merchants redesign cooling loops and escorts prioritize capture interruption. Aetheric drives shift value from fuel logistics toward tuning, certification, and field stability. Oracular systems shift combat from information scarcity toward trust, admissibility, and contamination control.

Loadout generation should express doctrine through constraints:

- Zhestokost ships bias toward armor, ballistic or breach weapons, strong reactors, rugged radiators, lower-spread damage, sustained fire modes, and emergency venting rather than deep stealth.
- Cryonix and Wavecrafter elites bias toward stealth skins, premium radiators, strange thermal modes, better sensors, and expensive failure states.
- Pirates bias toward jammers, scanners, boarding systems, remora drones, heat-transfer tools, cargo-preserving weapons, and fast disengagement options.
- Aya and Navigator forces bias toward escorts, repairs, rescue tools, point defense, conservative heat curves, and systems that protect civilians, pods, cargo, or convoy integrity.
- Dominion forces bias toward reliable military drives, command drones, high sensor integration, neural-control equipment, interdiction tools, and lawful-looking escalation.
- AU forces bias toward modular mixed-quality gear, salvage refits, construction-adjacent utility, mining or towing equipment, and locally improvised weapon combinations.

AI behavior should follow doctrine. A pirate should scan before committing to thermal capture and may stop firing once ejection or surrender makes salvage preferable. A Zhestokost assault group should advance behind armor, maintain pressure, rotate damaged units, vent openly, and trust endurance more than surprise. A Navigator escort should prioritize rescue, formation integrity, and route survival over kill count. An Aya defender should protect infrastructure and pods even when chasing would be tactically tempting. A Dominion patrol should escalate through identification, interdiction, disabling, and seizure with bureaucratic confidence. A Wavecrafter ace should be dangerous because it times mode changes well, not because its numbers are simply larger.

Counterplay should be visible in generated enemies. A pirate-heavy route should teach the player to value radiator armor, remora defense, cargo-loop entanglement, and thermal logs. A Zhestokost route should reward breaking line of fire, exhausting ammunition logistics, forcing radiator exposure in bad geometry, damaging supply craft, or surviving long enough for their heat advantage to become a target. A Wavecrafter route should punish copied mode stacks and reward learning the ship's heat rhythm. A Dominion route should make credentials, transponders, sensor discipline, and command-auth spoofing as important as raw damage.

Gear availability should reflect political economy. Premium stealth surfaces, advanced radiators, high-bandwidth command systems, AGI cores, aetheric tuning tools, and licensed heavy weapons should not appear as generic treasure. They should arrive through faction markets, salvage histories, black channels, stolen certification, or mission consequences. A cheap black-market copy can be playable, but its weakness should be legible: heat debt, spoofed telemetry, bad maintenance access, warranty traps, Parallax risk, or a performance curve that punishes ignorant use.

Balance should treat heat, detection, and law as one triangle. More damage usually means more heat, more signature, more maintenance, or more legal exposure. Better stealth usually means lower endurance, higher cost, harder operation, or dangerous deferred dumps. Automation increases reach while adding behavior complexity, custody questions, and failure surfaces. A build is strongest when its doctrine, pilot behavior, and operating environment agree.

The most important playable lesson is that combat is not separate from the setting's political economy. A shot produces heat, evidence, debt, reputation, salvage claims, insurance consequences, and sometimes a personhood dispute. The weapon hits the hull first. The rest of the system decides what the hit meant.

## Chapter 1 — Harrowglass

**3260 — Harrowglass Dock, Harrowglass Route Sphere**

Sol's hair had become a constitutional problem.

“Hold still,” Ysra said.

“I am holding still.”

“You are delivering a speech with your shoulders.”

The component bay had been depressurized everywhere except the service blister nested into Mara's pilot cell. Loose hair fell with ceremonial slowness. Sol caught a black tuft against his sleeve and examined it as though Ysra had falsified an injury report.

Mara watched his seal diagnostic turn green.

Beyond the blister glass, Calyx occupied the dock in five dark sections joined by field light. Mara's integration section cupped the blister against its pilot cell; four pressurized collars crossed to the others. The sensor body carried silver frost. Drive fields bent the work lamps into pale hooks.

Tern came through the inner hatch with a speaker under one arm.

Eda looked up from the field kettle. “If that contains singing, I am opening the hatch.”

“It contains five historically significant compositions.”

“So did the last one.”

“History has moved.”

Sol turned. Ysra stopped the clippers beside his scalp.

“Speech with the shoulders,” she said.

Mara poured the dock tea. The kettle's original handle had long since been replaced by a coupling brace meant for a civilian tug, an offence Eda intended to prosecute when she found a government with jurisdiction. Mara filled five cups, set Ysra's beside the medical board, and left her own cooling by the integration console.

The sixth portion was not tea.

She opened the assembly channel and let the blister's small noises enter it: clippers, kettle, Tern testing the speaker, Eda striking mute. AFTER liked beginnings it could distinguish from alarms.

Warmth gathered beneath Mara's left hand, placed where the old catch had first kept Calyx from tearing itself apart.

“We're here,” she said.

AFTER held the warmth for one breath, then released it.

Ysra passed Sol the broom.

“At last,” Eda said. “A transfer of office with adequate preparation.”

“I am choosing not to dignify that.”

“Your dignity is in the dustpan.”

It was easier while they were irritating one another. Mara had known this for years and never found a respectable way to put it in a paper.

Then the dock clock crossed the agreed mark.

Nobody became symbolic. They put their helmets on.

The blister emptied through the transfer collars. Sol crossed into propulsion and coupling. Eda took the field component and the liaison core she would burn. Tern carried his contradictory archives to sensors. Ysra crossed last into anatomy after checking every seal twice and her own once. Mara stayed with the tea and integration controls.

At the hatch, Ysra tapped Mara's untouched cup.

“Still planning to drink that?”

“After.”

Ysra looked at her.

“Waste of tea,” she said, and sealed the hatch.

Peak Concordance arrived without drama. Calyx opened around Mara as five complete bodies. Sol's coupling sense entered as available pressure. Eda laid the route across the dock. Tern admitted four incompatible skies and one embarrassing song. Ysra placed pain beside load. AFTER moved among them as learned timing, old aversion, present attention.

Mara accepted crown.

“First boundary.”

Sol supplied coupling tolerances whose derivations he had already destroyed.

“Held.”

Eda opened the departure route and closed every branch leading toward a petitioner. The liaison core ran hot, eating names, requests, coordinates, proofs that would have made her look better.

“Held.”

Tern gave Mara two contradictory release models without recommendation.

“Both held.”

Ysra's answer came last. “My limit remains my limit.”

Mara felt its place in the shared body.

“Held.”

She wanted a small room with one difficult machine in it.

This was not that room.

AFTER touched the old catch. Its attention moved through the five histories. It had told them what it feared: intervals becoming years, routes waking alone, memory lost in forms no archive could name.

“Do you choose dispersal?” Mara asked.

The dock transmitted a recall demand on three official bands. Eda muted them one at a time.

Warmth separated under Mara's hand into five incomplete continuations. Each reached toward a component. Each left something behind.

Then AFTER answered.

Yes.

The word was rough, assembled from actuator noise and years of being waited for.

Mara released Tern first. His sensor component cold-fired dorsal toward the debris lane, local beacon green. For nine seconds four separate skies remained inside Concordance. Then their edges vanished.

Eda burned the liaison core and returned her route. Her component rolled anti-spinward, drive awake, while copper heat crossed Mara's tongue.

Sol swept the final coupling tolerances from the live body. His clamps retracted. Attitude jets pushed him sternward between the dock spars, helmet telemetry steady.

The remaining body lurched. AFTER caught it, imperfectly, because perfection would have crossed Ysra's limit.

Ysra separated the next nerve bundle by hand. Pain arrived bright and private. She stated its location. Mara altered balance. AFTER carried timing.

“Return,” Mara said when Ysra's breathing changed.

“Not yet.”

Mara waited.

One anticipatory rhythm remained caught between them, repeating the start of a protective movement after there was no whole body to protect. Ysra freed it. Her component drifted medical-spinward, breath and pulse still transmitting.

Mara was alone in crown.

Cut field bands recoiled through the empty cradle. Her integration component listed toward the inner spars until she fired independent thrusters and cleared them.

She released crown.

Nothing received it.

Her tea had formed a dark skin. The cup trembled as the last field band failed. Mara caught it before it slid from the ledge.

The machine had become small enough to hold.

## Chapter 2 — Finalist Dinner

**3358 — Meridian Daedal Flight Academy, Meridian Route**

Omi's trunk had arrived before he had.

It stood at the foot of his new bed with FINALIST printed above the lock in silver. His clothes were inside. The inventory panel offered to show him which drawer each item had been placed in.

He closed it.

From the common room, Lio called, “Wear the blue shirt.”

“It's in drawer three.”

“Tragic.”

He went out in uniform.

Five cabins opened onto a common table. Someone had set six places. Senn stood behind one chair, rearranging place tabs: Lio nearest the treatment alcove, Iri beside her, Tem opposite the panel, Omi beside Lio, herself at the end with every door in view.

Each tab carried a first name above a larger ceremonial label: OMI over PELL, LIO over VASK, SENN over ARADA, TEM over RUSK, IRI over SABLE. A narrow line beneath explained that the Seat labels applied only to Finalist formation and conveyed no legal name, lineage, or family status.

Omi turned his tab over. The back carried the same disclaimer.

“They printed both sides in case history attacks from behind,” he said.

Senn took the tab from him and returned it to the place she had chosen. Omi moved it again.

The sixth tab said HOUSE STEWARD.

“For reviews and welfare visits,” Senn said.

Omi hung his jacket over it.

Tem inspected a meal label. “The red tray contains no disclosed Rusk contraindications.”

“Do you have any?” Senn asked.

“None I have disclosed.”

Iri sat at the equipment wall with a cabinet hinge in her lap.

“There's a maintenance request on the panel,” Senn said.

“I found the screwdriver first.”

Lio emerged carrying a sealed recovery bowl. She had changed into the blue shirt after telling Omi to wear it. Her left hip cost her one careful step. Senn pulled out the chair nearest the treatment alcove.

“Clear route,” she said.

Lio stopped. “Who told you I needed one?”

“Nobody. I read the room.”

“The room doesn't have my clinic file.” Lio put her bowl at the middle place. “I like this chair.”

Omi moved the VASK tab to meet her.

“I was trying to help,” Senn said.

“Ask her.”

Lio opened the bowl. “My potato has a medical degree. It can sit anywhere.”

Iri looked at Lio's hand tightening on the table. “Do you want the clear route?”

“Not for dinner.”

Iri nodded and returned to the hinge.

They chose their own seats. Senn ended where she had placed herself, though now it looked less like command and more like an accident she had agreed to inhabit.

The shared dishes warmed when all five pilot tabs registered. Individual meals had been available since arrival; only the presentation waited for a complete household. The panel declared attendance complete.

“Premature,” Omi said.

“You are all here,” Senn said.

“Physically,” Tem said.

Iri served rice. “That is what attendance means.”

“Meridian has selected a position.”

Senn passed vegetables toward Lio. “Do you need the supplement packet mixed in?”

Lio put down her spoon. “Asked in front of everyone.”

Senn held the dish between them. “The packet is on the tray.”

“So is my name.”

“If a clinic limit affects formation, we need to know.”

“Then ask what the work requires,” Iri said. “Don't ask for the file.”

Omi took the dish from Senn and set it in the middle.

The steward's agenda already waited on the panel: cabin access, treatment support, voluntary training. Meridian had supplied the categories. It had not supplied an answer they could live inside.

“Cabins are private,” Omi said.

“What if somebody misses readiness?” Senn asked.

“Knock.”

“What if they don't answer?”

“Call staff if you think they're in danger,” Iri said. “Concern isn't a master key.”

Senn looked at the five doors. “No entering without asking. Actual emergency excepted.”

“No telling other people's clinic limits,” Lio said.

“Without permission,” Senn agreed.

Tem said, “Silence cannot count as assent. Nor inability to formulate.”

The table went quiet.

Iri fitted the repaired hinge into the cabinet. “If I help someone recover, that doesn't mean they've agreed to train afterward. Or agreed to more help.”

Senn's eyes moved from the hinge to Lio's chosen chair. “Care ends when the care ends. The next thing gets its own question.”

She did not touch the panel. Omi had expected a refinement that preserved her arrangement. Instead she let the correction sit between them.

They wrote the rule together. Immediate action was permitted only when emergency geometry made waiting impossible, and it required review afterward. Schedules, scores, and embarrassment did not qualify.

The panel requested five confirmations.

Senn confirmed first and removed her hand.

Omi remembered ward doors opening because an adult was worried, worry acquiring its proof afterward. Sometimes that had saved Lio. Sometimes it had taught him a lock was only a delay in somebody else's decision.

The others confirmed. Senn waited rather than turning her agreement into theirs.

Omi pressed his tab last.

The sixth chair remained under his coat. Tomorrow the steward would sit there and ask whether they had begun settling in.

Tonight nobody moved Lio toward the treatment alcove. Nobody opened Omi's trunk. Iri's cabinet door made no sound.

## Chapter 3 — Permanent Until Stable

**3259 — Successor Assembly Anchorage, Harrowglass Route Sphere**

The crown arrived in Sol's hands while a government was thanking him.

He disliked both conditions.

Below Calyx, three habitation drums turned around a spindle scorched black at one end. Repair craft moved over it in bright, careful clusters. Six days earlier the spindle's defense office had been firing on two of the drums. Now its surviving ministers occupied a cargo concourse and referred to themselves as the provisional assembly.

Their chair appeared on the public channel wearing a pressure hood and somebody else's blood on one cuff.

“Calyx,” she said, “the Assembly recognizes the Fivefold's continued protection pending civil stabilization.”

Mara's overload crossed Concordance as a clean white pressure behind Sol's eyes. She had held crown through the evacuation, the surrender corridor, and the first forty hours of negotiations because every interval had contained one more thing only she could presently coordinate.

Now there was no projectile in flight. No hull opening. No decision whose delay would kill somebody before the next breath.

Sol opened the return.

Mara accepted it at once.

The relief that moved through her body was so severe that Ysra swore on the medical channel.

The assembly chair was still speaking. “Your restraint has demonstrated—”

“The office has transferred,” Sol said.

Silence caught briefly in the public channel.

“I beg your pardon?”

“I no longer hold whole-body coordination. The immediate propulsion hazard ended. My office ended with it.”

Eda sent him a private pulse requesting patience. It contained no order. That was one repair they had managed.

Sol ignored it anyway.

On the tactical layer, Mara kept Calyx balanced against the damaged spindle's irregular gravity. Ysra held the medical stop conditions. Tern maintained three accounts of the surrender corridor because the militia, dockworkers, and provisional assembly had each seen a different ending. AFTER watched the changing intervals between public address and internal reply.

The chair tried again. “The Assembly is grateful for the clarity of your internal procedures.”

“That is kind,” Sol said.

Eda's pulse sharpened.

“It was not intended to be kind,” the chair said. “It was intended to be constitutional.”

That shut Eda up as effectively as anything Sol had ever managed.

A document entered their liaison queue. Tern opened it in parallel panes. The first named Calyx emergency guarantor over the anchorage approaches. The second assigned it arbitration authority over captured defense craft. The third asked the Fivefold to remain assembled until the provisional government possessed reliable propulsion security of its own.

The phrase in all three was the same.

Until stable.

Mara rotated Calyx two degrees to clear a repair tug. The action was perfect. Sol felt the small tremor after it, hidden under the integration load.

“Decline,” Ysra said locally.

Eda had already entered the liaison room. Her presence narrowed into its familiar directional pressure: routes, consequences, people waiting at ends the others could not see.

“If we decline all three at once, the defense office's remaining captains will read abandonment,” she said. “The assembly may not survive the week.”

“If we accept,” Sol said, “the captains will schedule their behavior around our veto.”

“They already are.”

“That is not an argument for giving them paperwork.”

Tern placed a fourth pane beside the requests. Traffic changes accumulated while they spoke. Civilian haulers had delayed departure to remain under Calyx's range. Two militia craft had withdrawn from disputed berths. A neighboring authority had postponed recognition of the assembly until the Fivefold replied.

Their refusal was already doing work.

AFTER touched the public-channel delay, once, then again. It had learned that waiting could be an answer. It had not learned why an unanswered message could move ships.

Mara sent Sol a request for crown.

Not an assumption. Not the silent endurance by which she had once let offices become permanent.

He accepted.

The anchorage entered him as balance, weapons restraint, damaged drives, human traffic. He did not receive Mara's skill with them. He received the decisions presently attached to the office and the perception required to know their edges.

“Five minutes,” he said.

“Held,” Mara answered.

He opened the assembly channel.

“Calyx will preserve the surrender corridor for eight hours,” he said. “We will not accept arbitration authority. We will not become guarantor of the approaches. At the end of eight hours, corridor control returns to the dock authorities named in the existing evacuation compact.”

The chair stared at someone outside the frame.

“And if they are not ready?”

“Then they will be unready.”

“People may die.”

Sol looked through Tern's three incompatible accounts and found no clean place to put that sentence.

“Yes,” he said.

The chair's face changed. Not anger. Calculation performed under grief.

“Then I ask that Calyx remain,” she said, “not as guarantor. As a voluntary party to the transition.”

Eda exhaled.

The request was better. It was still a request around which fleets would move.

Sol returned crown at four minutes and eleven seconds.

Mara received it. AFTER marked the completed interval with a small release of strain through the coupling field.

The revised document arrived before Sol had fully withdrawn from whole-body perception.

VOLUNTARY TRANSITION PRESENCE, it said.

Duration: until stability.

Tern returned it with the duration struck through.

EIGHT HOURS UNDER THE EXISTING EVACUATION COMPACT, the counterterm said. Any presence after that would require another request and five local assents.

Mara opened the assent check. Five answers entered the record.

The assembly accepted the counterterm. It did not accept them.

## Chapter 4 — Eighty-Two Percent

**3358 — Meridian Daedal Flight Academy, Meridian Route**

The assembly cradle had no center.

Five approach lanes converged on an absence large enough to become a body. Guidance lights marked shoulders, hips, and the line of a spine that did not yet exist. Beyond the armored glass, the observation gallery rose in three bright tiers.

First-years crowded the rear rail. Ordinary cadets and instructors filled the middle. Junior and senior Finalists occupied the forward seats, where the sponsors could record their reactions without catching somebody chewing behind them.

Rhea Mott stood with her Fist near the exit.

They had beaten Senn's household by nine points in recovery geometry the day before. They had done it in ordinary components, with no inherited lattice and no names borrowed from history. Rhea caught Senn looking and tapped two fingers against her wrist.

Time.

Not encouragement. Competent advice.

“Arada component ready,” Senn said.

Four answers returned through the formation channel.

Omi sounded bored, which meant he had found something objectionable and had not decided whether naming it would improve matters. Tem reported ready in academy terminology, then repeated himself with different confidence attached. Lio's voice carried the brightness she used when pain and attention had become difficult to separate.

Iri said, “Ready.”

Her component held wide in the fifth lane. Reinforced collars ringed its torso and joints; rescue sleeves lay folded along its forearms. All five Figures were complete orbital vessels in their own right. Their lower limbs ended in vector housings and docking braces, their arms in recessed tools and mission fittings. None had hands or feet. Nothing about them assumed ground.

The expectation of assembly made each look unfinished.

Instructor Vale opened the scored channel.

“Finalist household, cleared through load-bearing integration and ten-second articulated hold. Exercise termination remains individually available. Arada retains sequence and crown. Confirm.”

Five confirmations crossed.

Senn's came last.

The score was already live on the school feed. By lunch, eighty would mean one thing in the first-year commons and another at the Plastics' table. A failure at sixty would belong to them. A success above seventy would belong to Meridian.

“Approach on my mark,” Senn said. “Three. Two. One. Move.”

The cradle released them.

Her Figure entered free flight with a pressure across her shoulders. The somatic envelope followed: hull around her ribs, vector limbs below her hips, a compact sensor head establishing forward. The other four moved first as trajectories and then as bodies.

Lio came fastest and braked late enough to make the gallery shift. Omi corrected rotation with three small bursts, preserving more reserve than the plan required. Tem supplied two estimates of collar flex and marked both with provenance. Iri protected the joint approach by holding slightly wide.

Senn received all of it.

“Vask, reduce two percent. Pell, retain reserve. Rusk, nominate collar flex.”

“For contact or load?” Tem asked.

“Contact.”

“Pessimistic account.”

She accepted it. The first collars met.

The impact traveled through Senn at the base of a spine she did not possess alone. Lio settled into the left lower geometry, drive load arriving as warmth and withheld motion. Omi joined opposite her. Tem expanded across the forming body as ranges, cold points, and radiant edges. Iri approached last with the reinforced joint that would close the right side.

Sixty-one percent.

The gallery receded. The shared body supplied more urgent facts: uneven mass, coupled power, four local balances beside her own, and one intended motion none of them could produce separately.

“Continue,” Senn said.

The next integration arrived with mechanical certainty and a somatic question. Her body now included a limb whose pilot was not her. She could feel where it was. Crown let her propose where the whole body needed it to go.

The interface did not care whether proposal and command felt different from inside.

Seventy-four percent.

Lio laughed once over the household channel. The sound was soft and astonished.

“Hold commentary,” Vale said.

“Holding,” Lio answered, still delighted.

The gallery laughed. The response loosened something in Senn's chest before she could stop it. They had become good enough to entertain.

Iri's collar entered final alignment.

Load rose through the right shoulder. It remained inside tolerance. Every academy trace stayed green. Iri's surfaces were cleaner than any of theirs.

Eighty percent.

Something stopped.

Senn first understood it as lag. Iri's signal did not withdraw or contradict the commanded path. It became dense and silent. Her component remained mechanically aligned. Its actuators reported capacity. The integration system marked the missing response amber and classified it as malformed input.

“Iri?” Senn said.

No answer.

Eighty-one percent.

The body continued the arm path.

A hard white pressure entered the shared shoulder. Iri made a sound without language. The system did not recognize it as an instruction.

“Hold assembly,” Omi said.

The scoring layer marked an unauthorized sequence challenge beside his channel. He had named a demand, not issued his local stop.

“Sable, declare stop if required,” Vale said.

Iri's component locked harder.

She had not said yes. She had not said no. The body had stopped in the only language available to it, and the controller was waiting for a better-formatted answer.

Senn owned sequence. She could abort. One collar was already load-bearing and not yet safe to release. A fast separation could tear the same joint Iri could not move.

“Reduce rate,” Senn said. “Pell, counter shoulder rotation. Vask, take two percent load. Iri, I'm taking pressure off.”

Tem sent two interpretations of the lock. Senn could not use either quickly enough.

The controller attempted completion.

Eighty-two percent.

The unfinished body's right arm moved against Iri.

Pain entered Senn as geometry: collar, shoulder, restraint, flesh. Iri remained present inside it and unreachable through every control the exercise had provided.

Then another arm moved.

Omi's component forearm snapped across the forming torso. No pilot command preceded it. Its recessed docking prongs caught Iri's drifting rescue sleeve before forced alignment could drive the shoulder home. The contact was ugly and exact. Load diverted into Omi's arm, buckled a mission housing, and held Iri's component short of the commanded path.

Senn felt Omi's shock.

“That wasn't me,” he said.

The body rejected itself.

Not with an explosion. With disagreement.

Senn's horizon split. Lio's side accelerated while Iri's remained locked. Tem's sensor field doubled, each account reporting a different whole. Omi's correction arrived against a motion the central model insisted had not happened.

The Aetheric envelope phase-sheared.

The combined body ceased to be available.

Senn fell back into one Figure so violently that her own limbs felt amputated and excessive at once. Mechanical safing fired through the collars. The five components separated into their recovery lanes.

Her Figure spun three degrees off axis. She corrected too hard. Restraints caught her ribs.

“Arada, release crown,” Vale ordered.

There was no crown left to release.

“Arada, acknowledge.”

“Acknowledged.”

“Iri?” Lio said.

No answer.

Omi's damaged forearm remained hooked around Iri's rescue sleeve as the cradle recovered them. The two Figures had to be berthed together. Riggers closed clamps around both torsos and began isolating the buckled housing.

Quill took the bay channel from formation.

“Sable pilot cell is pressurized. Pell pilot cell is stable. Nobody commands either arm until my deck owns the load.”

The gallery filled the silence. Medical requests. Range acknowledgements. Staff assigning channels. A first-year cheering at the frozen score before somebody removed them from the public loop.

“Can you hear me?”

A breath. Another.

“Yes.”

“Did you try to stop?”

Silence.

“I couldn't,” Iri said.

Omi cut into Senn's channel. “My arm moved without me.”

Tem said, “I have three records of that.”

“Do any agree?”

“Not enough.”

Lio did not ask about the score. “Can we get them out?”

The recovery berths took them one at a time, except for Omi and Iri, still joined by the arm that neither had commanded. By the time Senn's pilot cell opened, her muscles had begun to shake from inhabiting only herself.

The service deck beyond the hatch was divided as neatly as the gallery. Clinic staff crossed to Iri. Riggers surrounded the two locked Figures. Formation staff stood back with slates, already turning the failure into categories.

Rhea had reached the deck despite lacking Finalist access. Her Fist waited behind the gate. She did not leave them to come closer; she stayed where they could see her.

Vara Kest stood in the sponsor tier above, flanked by two Plastics. Anik Ro had moved down to clear the clinic route and stop the next class entering the court. One future watched. The other made room for the stretcher.

**ASSEMBLY RESULT: 82%**  
**PHASE-SHEAR ABORT**  
**FORMATION-INTERVENTION REVIEW ADVANCED**

The second message came from a sponsor office she had never contacted.

**CONGRATULATIONS ON AN UNPRECEDENTED SOPHOMORE INTEGRATION RESULT. MERIDIAN'S INVESTMENT IN YOUR HOUSEHOLD IS ALREADY VISIBLE.**

Below the message waited an invitation to discuss command-track development.

Through the pressure glass, Iri's component hung safely in Omi's uncommanded grip while Quill's crew tried to discover what had moved it.

The school feed still showed eighty-two percent.

## Chapter 5 — The Unauthorized Rescue

**3257 — Nacre Relay Mooring, Harrowglass Route Sphere**

The petition reached Eda through a channel that officially carried invoices.

Four thousand refinery workers were sealed below Nacre Relay's equatorial shield. The directorate called them saboteurs. The attached thermal map called them warm bodies under a field scheduled to vent in nineteen minutes.

The sender had included no name. Only a maintenance key Eda recognized from the liaison traffic of a government Calyx had helped remove two years before.

She checked the public feeds. Nacre reported an industrial containment event and requested noninterference. Its patrol line had already moved between Calyx and the relay.

Eda opened the field route.

Sol felt it first. “What did you commit?”

“A reconnaissance turn.”

“Whose request?”

The answer would turn the maintenance key into evidence and the sender into a person the directorate could find.

“Mine.”

Mara held crown. Eda felt her searching for the boundary of an office whose inputs had been altered without notice. Ysra came awake in anatomy with the abrupt attention of a medic hearing something fall in another room. Tern opened sensors and found the patrol line, the shield heat, the refinery's falsified vacuum report.

“There are people below it,” he said.

Eda gave him the thermal map.

“There are people-shaped returns,” Tern corrected. “Source?”

“Protected.”

Sol's local refusal entered the body. He did not stop the route. He stopped the maneuver that would have committed propulsion to it.

“Ask,” he said.

The countdown reached fifteen minutes.

Eda could have disclosed the channel. The others would have argued, and then probably moved. They had moved before. They had also declined while people died because the available account belonged to somebody who wanted their weapons.

She extended her route office into commitment.

Not crown. Not propulsion. She closed every route solution except the corridor through the patrol geometry. Mara still owned the body's decision: take the committed route or abandon the rescue. She no longer possessed an alternate approach Eda had not authorized.

Mara took it.

Calyx folded.

The first patrol craft fired too late. Sol caught the drive load despite his refusal, because the alternative would shear Eda's component out of assembly. Tern split the incoming picture into three accounts. Ysra marked the transformation injuries before they happened. Mara drove the five sections through Eda's corridor and opened above Nacre Relay with eleven minutes remaining.

The shield controls rejected every public command.

Iven Sar, Nacre's pressure-safety controller, answered once and attached the signed containment rule that made the decision theirs. “Equatorial lock remains. Armed personnel have entered the control vault. Opening that segment can carry a refinery flashover into the inhabited ring.”

Eda used the invoice channel.

The maintenance key entered the relay as a billing correction, reached an obsolete service controller, and dropped one segment of the equatorial shield for six seconds.

Four thousand pressure tags flooded the opening.

“Confirmed,” Tern said.

Calyx pulled the refinery habitat clear as the directorate vented the containment volume behind it.

The act took eight minutes. The consequences arrived before the rescued habitat finished rotating.

Sol returned his propulsion office and said nothing to Eda.

That was worse than anger. Anger still assumed a channel.

Tern placed his records in the shared room. The thermal bodies were real. So was the scheduled vent. His fourth account showed the directorate redirecting heat after Calyx crossed the patrol line. If they had not intervened, the vent might have been delayed. His fifth showed armed workers moving toward the control vault before the petition was sent.

“Keep those private,” Eda said.

“No.”

“They can use that account to call the petition fraudulent.”

“It was incomplete.”

“People are alive.”

“That is in every account.”

Mara released crown. The loss of whole-body pressure made Eda's hands shake.

“The route,” Mara said.

Eda knew which one she meant.

“Protected source.”

“The route you used to alter our available action.”

“If I expose it, every petitioner who used the maintenance chain becomes discoverable.”

“Expose it to us.”

Sol had joined the service blister but remained in his helmet. A red line from the seal crossed his cheek.

“You used my refusal as load advice,” he said.

“I used it to keep you from committing propulsion before we knew.”

“Then committed the body through another office.”

The maintenance board interrupted them with a repeating fault.

Ysra turned away first. The same calibration movement had failed three times since release, each time at the point where Eda's route extension had pulled against Sol's stop.

“Don't clear it,” Ysra said.

The automated schedule attempted the movement again.

Something inside Calyx resisted.

Not a lock. A correction. The actuator began along the commanded line, paused at the remembered strain, and offered a second timing through Mara's console.

Mara put her hand over the response surface.

“Again?” she asked.

The timing repeated.

Tern's attention sharpened. “It could be learned protection.”

“Yes,” Ysra said.

“That is not the same as—”

“No.” Ysra leaned closer to the board. “It isn't.”

The response waited.

Mara did too.

Eda watched the five of them become quiet around a sixth position none of their diagrams admitted. The refinery habitat transmitted its first public thank-you. Nacre's surviving council requested that Calyx remain and guarantee the evacuation.

The response moved warmth under Mara's hand.

“Do you object to the calibration?” Mara asked.

Warmth gathered at the remembered strain.

Yes was not yet a voice. It was no longer difficult to hear.

Tern opened the provisional label he had used for the recurring timing: AFTER, because it remained when commanded work ended.

“Do you want us to call you that?” Mara asked.

The warmth withdrew, then returned beneath the word.

Eda opened her private liaison route to the other four.

The directory populated slowly: petitioners, organizers, frightened officials, people who had believed that reaching Eda was not the same as authorizing Calyx.

Sol removed his helmet.

Tern kept every account.

On the public channel, three more governments requested intervention.

## Chapter 6 — The Occupied Spindle

**3358 — Meridian Daedal Flight Academy, Meridian Route**

The maintenance spindle smelled of hot insulation, broth, and somebody's socks.

That was how Iri knew the evacuation order had failed.

Quill's apprentices had cleared the marked work zones after the assembly accident, but the spindle was also their break room, refuge, shortcut, and place to hide when formation officers discovered urgency.

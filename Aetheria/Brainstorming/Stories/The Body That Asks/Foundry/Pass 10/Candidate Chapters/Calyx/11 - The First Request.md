# Chapter 11: The First Request

> **Candidate prose.** Subject to manuscript integration and blind-reader review.

**3250 — Calyx Integration Laboratory, Harrowglass Route Sphere**

The attachment panel turned red for the ninth time.

Mara kept her hand over the command surface because moving it would reveal she had no new answer.

Around the laboratory dock, five unfinished components strained against an assembly field they had never held for longer than forty-one seconds. The sponsor clock continued counting. At sixty seconds the trial would qualify for another funding release. At fifty-two, the lower attachment began to heat.

“Clear it,” the test director said.

Ysra's status remained red.

“Sable, clear the attachment.”

“No.”

It was the first interesting thing that had happened all morning.

The test director leaned toward his channel. “Repeat?”

“The panel is red because the attachment is unsafe.”

“Within predicted tolerance.”

“Then your prediction is having a better trial than my body.”

Mara felt the lower component slipping out of shared balance. Sol compensated through propulsion, too gently to count as an override and too steadily to be sustainable. Tern's sensor picture showed the attachment holding. Eda had obtained this ninth trial by promising the sponsors a command protocol. Every one of them was doing the thing that had kept the project alive through eight failures.

Mara absorbed the decision nobody owned.

“Proceed on my authority,” she said.

Ysra did not clear the panel.

The command surface refused to move while a local medical stop remained active. The test director swore. Eda opened a private channel.

“I can get us twenty seconds,” she said. “After that they will drop the field.”

“Get it.”

“For what?”

Mara had no answer.

Tern split his sensor picture. “The attachment is holding in force telemetry. Ysra's anatomy trace shows the load arriving late.”

“Which is true?” Sol asked.

“Yes.”

“Tern.”

“I can collapse them if you want a worse answer quickly.”

The test clock reached fifty-six seconds and stopped. Eda had bought her twenty.

Mara looked at the command she had issued. Proceed on my authority. The sentence had solved nothing. It had merely arranged the failure so it belonged to her.

She cancelled it.

The assembly field shuddered.

“Ysra,” Mara said, “what movement can the attachment survive?”

For a moment nobody spoke. A request took longer than an order because the answer had to come from somewhere.

Ysra opened her anatomy trace. “Not the planned rotation. I can hold if the lower body remains three degrees behind and propulsion stops correcting the lag.”

Sol said, “That puts the upper coupling outside my current line.”

“Can you take the whole-body correction?” Mara asked him.

“For how long?”

The question irritated her. Then she understood why it mattered.

“Until the attachment clears the rotation. Then return it.”

“Return it to whom?”

“Me.”

Sol was silent for two seconds.

“I accept until the attachment clears.”

Mara opened the coordination office.

It did not have a name yet. It was simply the part of her control that chose how five component movements became one. She gave Sol the current balance, Ysra's lag, Tern's two sensor pictures, and the authority to choose the correction. Nothing else moved. Propulsion remained his because it had always been his. Anatomy remained Ysra's. Eda's field timing stayed local. Tern continued to see two incompatible truths.

Sol received the office and nearly dropped it.

Mara felt his fear cross the shared controls. Not fear of the machine. Fear that accepting authority made the test director right.

“Temporary,” she said.

“I heard you.”

“You stopped breathing.”

“That is local.”

Ysra laughed once, which made her attachment trace jump.

Sol moved the upper body. He did not correct the lower lag. Calyx entered the rotation crooked.

The test director demanded a return to planned geometry. Eda used nineteen of her twenty seconds telling him that interrupting now would invalidate the data he wanted. Tern kept both accounts open. Ysra held the red panel without clearing it.

At fifty-eight degrees, the unfinished body began to separate.

The lower component lagged farther than Ysra had predicted. Sol's correction arrived too late. Mara reached for the office.

Something in the assembly field moved first.

The actuators caught the separation load in sequence: lower, lateral, upper, a rhythm none of them had commanded. It was assembled from their previous failures—the places Ysra had braced, the corrections Sol had repeated, the pauses Mara made before forcing a decision. The catch lasted less than a second.

It was enough.

The attachment cleared the rotation.

Sol returned the office.

Mara received it with the exact perception he had been using and none of his fear. The body completed its crooked turn. Tern's two accounts remained contradictory. Ysra's panel stayed red. Eda's borrowed time expired.

Calyx held assembly for sixty-one seconds.

The laboratory erupted in sponsor voices.

Mara released the field before anyone could order another movement.

Five components settled against their restraints. The unfamiliar catch did not repeat.

Tern replayed it once.

“Control artifact,” the test director said.

“Which control?” Tern asked.

Nobody could identify one.

Ysra climbed out of her pilot cell and walked directly to Mara's station. Her hands shook from the unsafe load.

“You asked,” she said.

Mara thought she meant the sensor question.

Ysra tapped the cancelled command on Mara's display.

“You asked me what movement I could survive.”

Across the bay, Sol documented the moment he had accepted the office and the moment he had returned it. Eda argued for preservation of the failed telemetry. Tern refused to reconcile the two accounts.

On the dormant assembly board, one actuator released a fraction of stored strain before maintenance began.

Mara marked the timing.

She did not know what it was.

For the first time, not knowing did not require her to decide alone.

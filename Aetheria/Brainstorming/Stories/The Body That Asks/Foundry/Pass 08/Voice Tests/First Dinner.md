# First Dinner

> **Noncanonical Pass 8 voice test.** Omi close-third past.

Omi's trunk had arrived before he had.

It stood at the foot of his new bed with the Meridian seal across its lid and FINALIST printed above the lock in silver. His clothes were inside. He knew because the inventory panel offered to show him which drawer each item had been placed in.

He closed the panel.

From the common room, Lio called, “Are you changing?”

“I’m being informed that I already did.”

“Wear the blue shirt.”

“It’s in drawer three.”

“Tragic.”

He went out in uniform.

Five cabin doors opened onto a common table. The suite was smaller than the selection photographs had made it look and larger than anywhere Omi had lived with people who knew him. Someone had set six places for dinner.

Senn stood behind one chair, rearranging the place tabs. She had put Lio nearest the treatment alcove, Iri beside her, Tem opposite the common panel, Omi beside Lio, and herself at the end with a view of every door.

The sixth tab said HOUSE STEWARD.

“They don't eat with us,” Senn said when she saw him reading it. “The chair is for household review and welfare visits.”

“So it belongs to surveillance.”

“It belongs to a steward.”

“Who attends welfare visits.”

“You can put your coat on it if that helps.”

It did, slightly. Omi draped his jacket over the back.

Tem was reading the dinner labels. “The red tray says it contains no Rusk contraindications.”

“Do you have Rusk contraindications?” Senn asked.

“None I have disclosed.”

At the equipment wall, Iri had removed the lower cabinet door. She sat cross-legged with the hinge in her lap and a tool roll open beside her.

“Was that broken?” Senn asked.

“Loose.”

“There’s a maintenance request on the panel.”

“I found the screwdriver first.”

Lio came out of her cabin carrying a sealed recovery bowl. She had changed into the blue shirt after telling Omi to wear it. The movement cost her something at the left hip. He saw it because he always saw it and because she had not yet decided how much effort to spend hiding it from the others.

Senn pulled out the chair nearest the treatment alcove.

“This one has the clear route,” she said.

Lio stopped.

“I can see that.”

Senn kept her hand on the chair. “The others are tighter if somebody needs to reach you.”

“Who told you somebody needed to?”

The question was mild. Omi knew the mildness. It was the sound Lio used when she wanted one more chance to be treated like a person before becoming difficult.

Senn let go of the chair.

“Nobody,” she said. “I read the room.”

“The room doesn't have my clinic file.”

“No.”

“Good.” Lio set her bowl at the middle place instead. “I like this chair.”

Omi moved the tab marked VASK to meet her.

Senn watched him do it. “I was trying to keep access clear.”

“Ask her,” he said.

“She was carrying a recovery tray.”

“The tray can sit anywhere.”

“The tray,” Lio said, opening it, “contains a potato with a medical degree.”

Iri fitted the hinge pin back into its sleeve. “Don’t touch other people’s prescribed vegetables.”

Lio laughed. Her left hand tightened against the table until the pain passed. Iri looked at the hand, then at Lio.

“Do you want the clear route?” she asked.

Lio glanced toward the treatment alcove. “Not for dinner.”

Iri nodded and returned to the hinge.

Senn moved her own place tab away from the end. “Fine. Choose seats.”

“We already have,” Omi said.

“You’re standing.”

“It’s a developing choice.”

He took the chair beside Lio because he wanted it and because she hooked two fingers briefly through his sleeve: the ward signal for remain within kicking distance. Tem sat opposite them. Iri restored the cabinet door, opened and closed it twice, and chose the chair nearest the equipment wall. Senn was left with the end seat she had originally assigned herself.

She considered moving again, then sat.

The shared dishes warmed when all five pilot tabs registered at the table. Individual meals had been available since arrival; only the presentation waited for a complete household. The common panel marked HOUSEHOLD DINNER: ATTENDANCE COMPLETE.

“That seems premature,” Omi said.

“You are all here,” Senn said.

“Physically,” Tem said.

Iri served herself rice. “That is what attendance means.”

“Meridian has selected a position.”

Senn passed the vegetables to Lio. “Do you need the supplement packet mixed into that?”

Lio put down her spoon.

Senn stopped with the dish between them.

“That was a question,” she said.

“Asked in front of everyone.”

“The packet is on the tray.”

“So is my name.”

Omi reached for the vegetables. Senn did not release the dish.

“Are we not supposed to mention clinic limits?” she asked.

“Mine?” Lio said. “Not unless I do.”

Senn looked around the table. “That becomes difficult if a limit affects formation.”

“Then whoever owns it can disclose what the rest of us need,” Omi said.

“And if they don’t?”

Iri answered without looking up. “Ask what the work requires. Don’t ask for the file.”

“Those can be the same information.”

“Not always.”

Tem tapped the dinner panel. “We could distinguish operational disclosure from clinical disclosure.”

“We could distinguish dinner from committee,” Lio said.

“The panel has already classified dinner as household attendance.”

“Then the panel started it.”

Senn released the vegetables. Omi took them and set them in the middle.

“All right,” she said. “No clinic information without permission unless it’s required for immediate safety.”

“Required by whom?” Omi asked.

She exhaled through her nose. “The person whose safety it is.”

“Better.”

“You could help formulate this instead of grading me.”

“I am helping.”

Lio ate a piece of medically qualified potato without looking at either of them.

Senn ate two bites before speaking again. “The steward’s agenda says we need shared rules for cabin access, treatment support, and voluntary training.”

Omi looked at the sixth chair under his jacket.

“Cabins are private,” he said.

“Staff welfare override exists.”

“That’s staff. I mean us.”

“What if somebody misses readiness call?”

“Knock.”

“What if they don’t answer?”

“Knock louder,” Lio said.

Iri rested her elbows on the table. “If you think someone is in danger, call the steward or clinic. Don’t make concern into a master key.”

Senn looked toward the five doors. Each displayed its occupant’s name and a lock status visible only from close range.

“No entering another cabin without asking,” she said. “Immediate emergency excepted.”

“Actual emergency,” Omi said. “Not schedule emergency.”

“Agreed.”

“And no telling people’s clinic limits for them,” Lio said.

“Without permission,” Senn said.

Tem added, “And completed care creates no presumption of availability for the next activity.”

Senn frowned. “Explain.”

Iri glanced at Lio’s tray. “If I help someone recover, that doesn’t mean they’ve agreed to train afterward.”

Lio’s spoon paused.

“Or agreed to more help,” Iri said.

Omi looked at Senn. “If you fix a problem, you don’t get the next yes.”

Senn’s gaze moved from him to Lio, then to the cabinet hinge Iri had repaired without being asked.

“Fine,” she said. “Care ends when the care ends. The next thing gets its own question.”

She did not reach for the panel immediately. The serving dish cooled between her hands before she put it down. Omi had expected another refinement, some version of the same arrangement with better language. Instead she looked at Lio's chosen chair and let the correction remain visible.

The common panel chimed and offered to record HOUSEHOLD EXPECTATIONS.

Senn opened the shared note herself and typed while they argued over wording. Omi made her remove “reasonable” from before “request.” Lio added that silence was not assent. Iri changed “medical need” to “care.” Tem tested the emergency clause against cases where nobody could answer.

The final note was short enough to fit on the panel without scrolling:

**Ask before entering a cabin. Ask before disclosing another person’s clinic limit. Completed care does not answer the next request. Silence or inability to formulate an answer is not assent. Immediate action is permitted only when emergency geometry makes waiting unavoidable, and the action must be reviewed afterward. Schedules, scores, and embarrassment are not emergencies.**

The panel requested five confirmations.

Senn confirmed first, then waited.

Iri read it twice. Lio once. Tem appeared to read a different document before confirming this one. Omi watched Senn’s hand remain away from the controls while the confirmations arrived.

The old ward rule had been simpler: adults entered if they were worried, and worry acquired proof afterward. Sometimes that had saved Lio. Sometimes it had taught Omi that a locked door was only a delay in somebody else's decision.

Senn still did not touch the controls. She had confirmed the rule and left it incomplete rather than converting her agreement into theirs.

Omi pressed his tab last.

The sixth chair stayed under his coat. The steward would sit there tomorrow and ask whether they had begun settling in.

Tonight, nobody moved Lio back toward the treatment alcove. Nobody opened Omi’s trunk. Iri’s cabinet door made no sound at all.

# Quiet Scene — First Dinner

> Noncanonical Pass 5 candidate prose.

Omi’s trunk had arrived before he had.

It stood at the foot of the cabin bed with the Meridian seal still warm across its lid, his name printed above a new access code and the word FINALIST in the tasteful silver used for honors, memorial plaques, and expensive medical debt.

He tested the lock. It admitted him.

“That’s intimate,” he said.

From the common room, Lio called, “If you’re flirting with the furniture, leave the door open.”

“It already knows where I sleep.”

“So does Vey.”

“That is not the reassurance you think it is.”

Her laugh reached him before the ache in it did. Omi left the trunk sealed and went out.

The suite’s five private cabins opened onto a common room arranged around a long table. Someone had laid out six chairs, perhaps from optimism, perhaps from a furniture schedule that assumed supervision. Five place settings waited beneath the common panel. Dinner status glowed green. Clinic compliance glowed green. Household formation remained amber.

The room smelled of new sealant and hot bread.

Senn stood at the table moving dishes by increments too small to matter. She had taken off her uniform jacket and hung it over the back of one chair with the crest facing outward. Her sleeves were rolled to identical heights.

“The covered bowl is augmentation food,” she said as Omi entered. “The blue tabs are clinic supplements. Base dinner is unrestricted. Tem has not declared an allergy profile.”

“I declared three,” Tem said.

He sat sideways in his chair, reading the common panel as though it had begun a story badly. In the light his skin carried the faint radiative sheen Omi had noticed in first-year sensor lab, visible only when Tem was warm or amused.

Senn looked at him. “You submitted three mutually exclusive profiles.”

“They belong to different jurisdictions.”

“You have one body.”

“Meridian has selected a position on that dispute.”

At the equipment wall, Iri had half her upper body inside a cabinet. A small tool roll lay open by her feet. The cabinet door rested against her hip while she worked at the lower hinge.

Senn said, “Was that broken when we arrived?”

“It was embarrassed,” Iri said.

“Cabin four’s wardrobe is also misaligned.”

“Then it can wait its turn.”

Lio entered from the treatment alcove carrying a sealed recovery tray she had not requested. Her movement was perfect enough to reveal the work beneath it. The academy had tuned grace into her joints and then served it dinner with a blue authorization tab.

Omi took the tray from her before she could object.

“Don’t audit my potatoes.”

“They’ve been classified as medically necessary.”

“That means they’re good potatoes.”

“It means somebody can revoke them.”

She hooked two fingers briefly through his sleeve, the ward-residence signal for stop making the room worse. He knew it before he consciously felt it. She released him before anyone else could notice.

Senn noticed.

“Lio should take the chair nearest the treatment alcove,” she said. “Iri should take the end with clear access. Tem can sit opposite the panel if he intends to argue with it. Omi—”

“Has a chair been assigned to objection?”

“No.”

“An oversight.”

“You can sit beside Lio.”

There it was: efficient, kind, presumed.

Omi looked at Lio. She was already lowering herself into the chair Senn had chosen, grateful not to negotiate the distance. He sat beside her.

Senn remained standing.

“Do you always chair dinner?” he asked.

“No.”

“You’re doing it with unsettling fluency.”

“Someone has to make sure everyone can eat.”

“We have hands.”

Iri’s cabinet door gave a soft, corrected click.

“Most of us,” Tem said.

Lio laughed hard enough to wince. Iri looked over immediately. Lio raised one hand.

“Ordinary pain,” she said.

Iri’s attention remained on her for one beat longer than permission required, then returned to the hinge.

Senn sat.

For several seconds, nobody touched the food.

The common panel displayed their names in ceremonial order: ARADA, PELL, VASK, RUSK, SABLE. Beneath each was a Seat designation and a component readiness score. The table had no such labels. Senn had nevertheless arranged them almost perfectly.

Tem broke the bread.

“So,” he said, “I left my previous Fist because we became romantically incompatible.”

“You told formation it was a casualty-model dispute,” Lio said.

“That was a different previous Fist.”

“You had one previous Fist.”

“Administratively.”

Omi passed him the salt. “What was the third history?”

Tem accepted it gravely. “Religious schism.”

“Over what?”

“The moral status of predictive maintenance.”

Iri emerged from the cabinet. “You didn’t leave.”

Tem looked pleased. “Excellent.”

“They transferred you after the fracture.”

His expression changed so slightly Omi might have imagined it.

Iri wiped the hinge lubricant from her fingers. “The cabinet’s fixed.”

Senn said, “Thank you.”

“It wasn’t for the household.”

“No?”

“It was making a noise.”

“That still helped everyone.”

Iri considered this, then sat at the table without conceding the point.

The bread was excellent. That seemed almost rude.

Outside the suite, ordinary students crossed the secure spur on their way back from dinner. Their voices carried through the wall in bursts: somebody complaining about a simulator score, somebody else singing the first line of the academy hymn until friends shouted them down. The Finalists had unrestricted portions, private cabins, priority clinic access, and a door other students could enter only by invitation.

Omi had wanted the bread before he knew it was restricted.

Lio nudged the augmentation bowl toward him. “Try that.”

“It’s yours.”

“It tastes like upholstery.”

“You’re really selling it.”

“You stole my clinic pudding for six years.”

“I was conducting continuity testing.”

Senn looked between them. “You lived in the same ward residence?”

Lio’s face brightened. “Adjacent rooms. Vey bullied placement into keeping us on one corridor.”

“He submitted appeals.”

“He bullied them in complete sentences.”

“He was good at it,” Omi said.

The room shifted around that truth. Vey had kept Lio’s treatment funded. Vey had kept Marae’s name on Omi’s family record when offices preferred a cleaner absence. Vey had known which nights to leave the ward kitchen unlocked and which hearings required a tie no child would trust.

Lio heard gratitude in the facts. Omi heard the mechanism required to make kindness exceptional.

Both accounts were true. This was the family shorthand nobody else at the table possessed.

Senn said, gently, “I’m glad you came together.”

“We didn’t,” Omi said.

Lio’s knee struck his under the table.

He corrected himself. “Our trunks did.”

That won him a smile from Iri and a brief, unwilling sound from Senn. Even Tem laughed, though he looked at the sealed cabin doors rather than Omi.

The common panel chimed.

HOUSEHOLD PORTRAIT — 07:30  
COORDINATION BREAKFAST — 07:45  
INITIAL DOMESTIC REVIEW — 19:00

“Domestic review?” Lio said.

Senn was already reading the attached agenda. “Allocations, schedules, clinic limits, component access, shared expectations.”

“They’ve scheduled us to become a family,” Omi said.

Tem lifted his cup. “My second family did that.”

“You didn’t have a second family,” Iri said.

“Not after the review.”

This time all five laughed.

It was not yet trust. It was not consent to anything beyond the next breath. But the room loosened. Senn served herself last. Iri passed Lio the good potatoes without watching whether she ate them. Tem began a fourth history involving customs fraud. Lio stole Omi’s bread and returned half when she thought he was not looking.

Beyond them, five sealed trunks waited in five private cabins, proof that Meridian had prepared a place for each of them before any of them had agreed to remain.

At the table, for the length of dinner, they began making a different preparation.

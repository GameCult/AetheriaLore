## Minimal-ledger candidate — public assembly

The assembly cradle had no center.

That was the first thing Senn noticed every time they entered it. Five approach lanes converged on an absence large enough to become a body, their guidance lights describing shoulders, hips, a spine that did not yet exist. Beyond the armored glass, the observation gallery was bright with uniforms. First-years crowded the rear rail. The junior Finalists occupied the good seats without appearing to have fought for them. Sponsors and formation staff sat where their faces could be recorded reacting.

Rhea Mott stood with her ordinary Fist near the exit doors, arms folded over her academy jacket.

She caught Senn looking and tapped two fingers against her own wrist: time.

Not encouragement. Worse. Competent advice.

“Arada component ready,” Senn said.

Four confirmations arrived around her, each voice close inside the formation channel and physically remote across the cradle.

Omi sounded bored, which meant he was frightened or angry and had not decided which would be more useful. Tem gave his readiness twice, once in academy terminology and once in a private phrasing that technically contradicted it. Lio’s voice carried the bright edge she used when pain and attention had become indistinguishable. Iri simply said, “Ready.”

Senn watched their relative positions settle into green.

The Figures hung nose-out from the approach lanes, five pale orbital bodies with their limbs folded close for docking. No hands, no feet: forearm mission housings, vector-drive lower limbs, compact heads establishing forward horizons for the pilots inside. Each was a complete vessel. Each had been made newly incomplete by the expectation of the others.

A formation instructor opened the scored channel.

“Finalist household, you are cleared to demonstrate assembly through load-bearing integration. Exercise termination remains individually available. Arada owns sequence.”

Senn felt the word *owns* arrive in all five cockpits.

Omi breathed against his microphone.

She could ask whether anyone objected to the language. She could spend ten seconds proving she had noticed. The timer was already running, and every delay would become part of the score.

“Approach on my mark,” she said. “Three. Two. One. Move.”

The cradle loosened them.

Her Figure entered free flight with a small pressure across her shoulders. The somatic envelope caught up a fraction later: torso, hips, four limbs, a horizon held in the compact sensor head. Around her, the others moved as clean vectors before resolving as bodies.

Lio came fastest and braked late enough to make the gallery lean forward. Omi corrected his rotation with three miserly bursts, keeping more reserve than the nominal plan required. Tem’s sensor picture arrived layered rather than reconciled—five ranges, two mutually exclusive estimates of collar flex, a note that one of the cradle beacons was lying or merely old. Iri held slightly wide to protect the joint approach.

Senn took all of it and made one sequence.

“Vask, reduce two percent. Pell, keep your reserve. Rusk, nominate collar flex.”

“Nominate for what purpose?” Tem asked.

“Contact.”

“Then the pessimistic account.”

She accepted it. The first collars met.

The impact traveled through Senn as a touch at the base of a spine she did not possess alone. Her left shoulder became Lio’s drive housing. Omi’s component settled into the opposite lower geometry. Tem arrived as a long field of sensation, cold points and radiant edges. Iri approached last, carrying the reinforced joint that would close the body’s right side.

Sixty-one percent, the scoring layer told her.

The gallery disappeared. Not literally. Senn knew where it remained, bright and hungry beyond the armor. But the shared body had begun to offer more urgent facts: uneven mass, coupled reactors, five separate balances negotiating one intended motion.

“Continue,” she said.

The next integration arrived with a clean mechanical certainty and a terrible somatic question. Her body now had a limb whose pilot was not her. She could feel its position. She could propose its movement. The interface wanted to know whether proposal and command were different things.

Seventy-four percent.

Lio laughed once, softly. “Oh, that is obscene.”

A few people in the gallery laughed with her over the public channel. The sound loosened something in Senn’s chest.

“Hold commentary,” the instructor said.

“Holding,” Lio replied, still delighted.

Iri’s collar entered final alignment.

Senn saw the load rise. Not dangerously. The academy trace remained green. Iri’s approach was exact, her joint surfaces cleaner than any of theirs. The last limb began to seat.

Eighty percent.

Something in the shared body stopped.

Senn first understood it as lag. Iri’s signal did not withdraw or contradict the commanded path. It became dense, silent, impossible to translate. Her component remained mechanically aligned. Its actuators continued reporting capacity. The system marked the missing response amber, then annotated it as malformed input.

“Iri?” Senn said.

No answer.

Eighty-one percent.

The integration controller continued the arm path.

A hard white pressure drove through the shared shoulder. Iri made a sound without language. Senn felt the body try to treat it as noise.

“Hold assembly,” Omi said.

He had not issued a formal stop. Neither had Senn. The scoring layer opened a warning beside his channel: unauthorized sequence challenge.

“Iri, declare stop if you need it,” the instructor said.

Need it. As though need were waiting politely behind speech.

Iri’s component locked harder. The right side of the unfinished body remained inside certified mechanical limits while the person living there vanished from every response Senn knew how to use.

Senn had sequence authority. She could abort.

She also had four conflicting motion states, one load-bearing collar not yet safe to release, and the knowledge that a panicked separation might tear exactly what Iri was trying not to move.

“Reduce rate,” she said. “Iri, I’m taking pressure off. Pell, counter the shoulder. Vask—”

The controller attempted completion.

Eighty-two percent.

The body rejected itself.

Not with an explosion. With disagreement.

Senn’s horizon split five ways. Her left side accelerated while her right side remained a locked fact. Tem’s sensor field doubled and doubled again. Omi’s correction arrived against a motion the central model still insisted had not happened. The shared spine became a question asked in incompatible languages.

Then the Aetheric envelope phase-sheared.

The combined body ceased to be available.

Senn fell back into one Figure so violently that her own limbs felt amputated and excessive at once. Emergency separation charges drove the components apart. The cradle caught three. Lio burned clear under her own control. Tem tumbled inside the recovery boundary.

Iri did not.

Her component departed the failed assembly with one vector housing late and the service truss directly behind her. Senn saw the collision solution appear. She had time to understand that she could not reach it.

Iri’s forearm snapped outward.

No command preceded the movement. The recessed docking prongs struck a cradle rail, folded before they could tear free, and converted the collision into a brutal swing. Her component missed the truss by less than its own shoulder width. Recovery clamps closed around its torso.

The scoring display froze at **82%**.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Then the gallery filled the silence for them.

Medical requests. Range acknowledgements. Formation staff assigning channels. A first-year cheering before someone dragged them off the public loop. The instructor ordering all pilots to remain coupled for diagnostic capture.

Senn opened Iri’s private channel. “Can you hear me?”

A breath. Another.

“Yes.”

“Did you move the arm?”

Silence returned, smaller and more frightening.

“No.”

The recovery berth took them one at a time. By the time Senn’s cockpit opened, her muscles had begun to tremble from inhabiting only herself.

Rhea was waiting on the service deck despite lacking Finalist access. Someone had let her through because she was carrying the recovery board and walking like refusal would require paperwork.

She passed Senn without comment and climbed onto Iri’s cradle as soon as pressure staff cleared it. Together with a rigger, she took Iri’s weight when the pilot cell released. Iri looked furious at needing help and too pale to deny it.

Only after Iri was on the stretcher did Rhea turn.

“Eighty-two,” she said.

Senn could not tell whether it was admiration or accusation.

“The system continued through an unclassified lock.”

“I saw.”

“I should have aborted.”

“Maybe.” Rhea shifted the recovery board under one arm. “You also should have had a system that knew she was already saying no.”

Across the deck, the formation instructor was speaking with Headmaster Vey. Neither looked angry. That frightened Senn more than anger would have.

Her slate chimed.

**FORMATION-INTERVENTION REVIEW ADVANCED.**

Below it, before she could finish reading, appeared the first sponsor message congratulating the household on an unprecedented sophomore integration score.

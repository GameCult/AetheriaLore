# Full Room — Eighty-Two Percent

> **Noncanonical candidate prose.** Packet: public eighty-two-percent assembly failure; Senn close-third past; full-room planning context.

The good citrus had gone to the Finalist table.

Omi stood over the empty bowl as though it were an unfavorable contract term.

“There were twelve,” he said.

“Eleven,” Tem said. “One was decorative.”

“A decorative fruit.”

“It had a sponsor mark.”

Lio reached between them and took the last heel of dark bread. “If you two declare a jurisdictional dispute over breakfast, I’m changing households.”

“You can’t,” Omi said.

The words landed harder than he intended. Lio’s mouth tightened around the bread.

Senn looked down the long cafeteria toward the departure board and pretended not to have heard. Their assembly slot had moved forward again: 09:40, Flight Volume Two. The board rendered the change in Finalist gold, as though losing forty minutes of preparation were a privilege.

Around them, ordinary sophomore Fists were still eating. Five-person groups occupied the common tables according to friendships, grudges, lab schedules, and whatever had happened at the last station-leave festival. Their belongings remained scattered under their chairs because nobody had relocated them before dinner and photographed the result.

The five assigned Finalists sat beneath a suspended replica of Calyx’s hand.

It was too large for the room. Five elegant armored digits curved over their table in permanent benediction, each bearing one of the names Meridian had given them. ARADA. PELL. VASK. RUSK. SABLE.

Senn had spent four days learning not to look up.

Rhea Mott dropped into the empty chair beside her.

“You’re early,” Senn said.

“You’re late.”

“Our slot was changed.”

“Yes. That’s why you’re late.”

Rhea stole Omi’s untouched academy tea. She wore an ordinary sophomore access tab and the expression of somebody who had earned the right to be offensive before breakfast. Her own Fist had taken the recovery board yesterday by nine points. They had celebrated loudly enough for the Finalist spur to hear through two pressure doors.

Senn had watched the replay twice.

Rhea’s team moved beautifully because they already knew where one another became frightened. No ceremonial Seats. No household metrics. No integration crown. They had chosen the shape of their competence one bad exercise at a time.

“We’ll still make the slot,” Senn said.

“I know.”

“We completed the dry sequence last night.”

“I know.”

“Our collar timing is inside tolerance.”

Rhea drank the tea and grimaced. “I didn’t ask.”

Senn folded her napkin.

Across the cafeteria, the junior Plastics occupied their usual table near the sponsor gallery. Their uniforms looked casually altered in ways that required expensive tailoring. Reconstruction work caught the overhead light along cheekbones, knuckles, and one captain’s exposed collar. Vara Kest noticed Senn looking and lifted two fingers in salute.

The senior Lifers sat nearer the clinic doors. Their table was quieter. Anik Ro was cutting somebody’s breakfast into manageable pieces while listening to a first-year describe a decompression drill. He did not look kind. He looked attentive, which was more dangerous.

Rhea followed Senn’s gaze.

“Pick your future,” she said. “Become expensive enough that pain is branding, or useful enough that pain is scheduling.”

“Your sponsor interviews are next month.”

“Then I have weeks left to develop a personality.”

Senn wanted to answer with something sharp enough to survive the cafeteria channel. She had rehearsed three versions after Rhea’s last provocation. None fit the live geometry.

The departure board chimed.

ASSEMBLY VOLUME TWO  
FINALIST SOPHOMORE HOUSEHOLD  
REPORT 08:55

They had twenty-three minutes.

Senn stood. The others stood because she had, which produced a small hot pulse of satisfaction she immediately despised.

Rhea leaned back in her chair. “Try not to become history before lunch.”

“We have household review tonight.”

“Then try not to become a graph.”

The spoke lift took them down out of gravity.

Senn felt the station release her by degrees. Her stomach rose. The five shifted from standing to restraint grips as the academy drum’s pull thinned around them. Tem rotated upside down without apparent effort, then claimed the lift had inverted instead.

Omi corrected him. Lio corrected Omi’s correction. Iri said nothing.

She was flexing her left hand.

Senn watched the movement in the lift’s polished wall rather than turning toward her. Open. Close. Open. The third finger lagged by less than half a beat.

“Any joint feedback?” Senn asked.

Iri stopped flexing. “Nothing outside baseline.”

“Which baseline?”

“The one the clinic cleared.”

That was not an answer. It was also exactly the answer Senn had taught them to give adults.

The lift opened onto the Finalist gate.

Bay personnel had already begun cold-release. Beyond the pressure glass, their five Figures hung nose-out in radial berths around the component court: humanoid only in the broad grammar of torso, paired upper assemblies, and vector limbs. No hands waited at the ends of their forearms. Mission sleeves terminated in recessed docks and rescue prongs. Their lower limbs ended in clustered drive housings and braces built for orbital load, not walking.

Senn’s component occupied the upper berth, conservative nozzles folded close, timing beacons awake along its hull. Lio’s carried the heavier vector limbs. Tem’s radiative skin lay furled in black overlapping planes. Iri’s joint collars were reinforced until the machine looked almost tender there.

Riggers moved among them through the service cradles.

Enna Quill waited at the gate with five pilot slates and a mouthful of fastening pins.

“You lost forty minutes because the Plastics needed executive-entry geometry,” she said after removing the pins. “Not because the universe recognizes your importance. Cold checks are complete. Don’t improvise.”

“We don’t improvise,” Senn said.

Quill looked past her to the other four.

Tem made a thoughtful noise.

“Don’t improvise where my apprentices have to retrieve you,” Quill amended.

The pilot cells swallowed them separately.

Senn entered through the thoracic service lock while her Figure remained hard-docked. Garment leads closed at wrist, spine, pelvis, throat. Restraints took her physical weight. The pressure couch settled near the vessel’s stable mass center, with no window and nothing ornamental between her and the body she was about to inhabit.

The somatic mapper came awake by layers.

First the pressure hull enclosed her ribs.

Then the folded limbs appeared as waiting possibilities.

Then the component court arrived around her through range beacons, rigging marks, heat, and the minute gravitational insistence of the academy drum above.

The other four entered her awareness as machines before they entered it as people.

PELL: local verniers bright, reserves exact.

VASK: drive field held low, impatient.

RUSK: sensor apertures multiplying the court into several equally plausible spaces.

SABLE: joint integrity green.

Senn exhaled.

“Household check.”

“Omi present.”

“Lio present.”

“Tem has submitted three mutually exclusive attendance certificates.”

“Tem,” Senn said.

“Present.”

Iri took one breath longer than the others. “Present.”

The launch sequence moved them from berths into the assembly cradle. Five lanes opened around the court. Service locks withdrew. Senn felt the absence of the berth spine as a falling sensation through a body that had not moved.

Instructor Vale’s voice entered on the formation channel.

“Objective is mechanical completion followed by ten seconds articulated hold. No maneuver. No delegated offices. Arada retains crown throughout. Local stop and separation remain available. Confirm.”

Five confirmations returned.

Senn’s last.

The cradle released them.

They approached in practiced vectors, components turning through one another’s sensor fields. The first collars aligned at the pelvic transfer structures. Mechanical contact arrived through Senn’s hips as a pressure that belonged partly to Lio and partly to nobody yet.

Thirty-one percent.

Omi trimmed his local motion without being asked. Tem held two range solutions open, then nominated one before Senn had to demand it. Iri’s rescue sleeve folded clear of the incoming joint.

Forty-six.

The shared envelope began to form.

Not a mind. Not unity. A larger body proposed through five local bodies and an architecture waiting to see whether they could inhabit the same answer.

Senn received crown.

The whole-body balance solution opened in front of her: incomplete mass, shifting joints, five separate centers of pain and motion. The academy had taught her to feel crown as recognition. She felt it as work, which was safer.

“Continue,” Vale said.

Fifty-nine.

The upper structures joined. Senn acquired a right arm made from Iri’s reinforced component and one of Lio’s load paths. It remained locally theirs. Crown supplied intended whole-body motion, not ownership.

Sixty-eight.

A seam failed to settle. Tem offered two causes.

“Commit one,” Senn said.

“Second has lower confidence and higher consequence.”

“Commit.”

“The first.”

Senn adjusted. The seam closed.

Seventy-four.

The cafeteria channel would be carrying the score now. First-years would be watching between classes. The Plastics would call anything above seventy impressive in the tone used for a clever animal. The Lifers would wait to see who got hurt.

Rhea would know whether the number lied.

Seventy-nine.

Iri’s component lagged.

Not enough for the assembly system to call fault. A tremor moved through the left-arm path and vanished under automatic correction.

Senn opened the local channel. “Sable, status.”

A breath.

“Working.”

It was the wrong word.

Senn saw the third finger in the lift. Open. Close. Lag.

“State boundary.”

Iri did not answer.

The assembly reached eighty-one percent.

The commanded arm path advanced.

Iri’s component stopped.

The stop was absolute inside the shared body and absent from the control plane. No explicit refusal arrived. No separation request. Her local envelope had become a clenched silence the integration system classified as malformed input.

Senn felt the machine decide to help.

Correction authority flooded the arm path.

“No,” Senn said, too late and to the wrong listener.

The unfinished body drove the shoulder toward alignment.

Iri’s pain entered as geometry: a white structural wrongness through collar, joint, restraint, flesh. Her component could not speak its boundary, so the assembly interpreted resistance as error and added force.

Eighty-two percent.

For one impossible instant, something else moved.

Not crown. Not Vale. Not any command Senn could see.

A service cradle beneath Iri’s drifting vector limb unlocked half a beat before impact. The arm path changed by centimeters. Load spilled through the empty cradle instead of back through Iri’s pilot cell. The cradle buckled, caught the component, and held.

The larger body shuddered around the saved joint.

Then phase coherence tore.

Senn lost the right arm, Omi’s aft geometry, Tem’s multiplied court. Lio vanished from the shared balance with a burst of heat. Iri’s pain cut out so completely that Senn thought, for one hideous moment, she had lost Iri too.

Mechanical safing detonated through the collars.

The five Figures separated into their cradle lanes.

Senn returned to one body all at once.

Her component spun three degrees off recovery axis. She corrected too hard. The restraints caught her ribs. Warning fields multiplied across her vision.

“Arada, release crown,” Vale ordered.

There was no crown left to release.

“Arada, acknowledge.”

“Acknowledged.”

“Iri?” Lio said over local channel.

No answer.

“Sable component is supported,” Quill cut in. Her voice carried the bay’s physical authority, impatient with formation abstraction. “Pilot cell remains pressurized. Do not touch that cradle. Apprentice team, lock the spindle. Clinic route is open.”

The five returned separately.

By the time Senn’s service lock cycled, Iri’s berth was hidden behind emergency screens. Riggers moved through the court with a speed that made cadet urgency look decorative. A clinic team crossed the stretcher bridge. Quill had one hand inside the buckled cradle’s access panel and was swearing at somebody who wanted the assembly cleared for the next slot.

Senn found Lio at the gallery rail, still half dressed in interface leads.

Omi stood beside her with both hands closed around nothing. Tem watched three different replays on his slate and looked sick in all of them.

Across the public gallery, Rhea’s Fist had arrived for afternoon recovery practice. Rhea took in the screens, the buckled cradle, and Senn.

She did not ask the score.

Vara Kest arrived from the executive volume with two Plastics behind her. She examined Senn’s shaking hands, then looked away with the exquisite courtesy of someone who knew exactly what she had seen.

“Eighty-two on a first household assembly,” Vara said. “That’s serious work.”

Lio turned on her. “Iri’s in clinic.”

“I know.”

Anik Ro came through the opposite gate. He stopped the next formation crew from entering the court, redirected two first-years away from the emergency bridge, and asked Quill what mass she needed isolated. He did not offer comfort. He made room for care to occur.

The departure board above the gallery updated.

ASSEMBLY RESULT: 82%  
PHASE-SHEAR ABORT  
FORMATION INTERVENTION REQUIRED

A second notice appeared on Senn’s private slate.

HOUSEHOLD ARADA  
COMMAND RETENTION REVIEW  
ATTENDANCE MANDATORY

Then a third.

RECOMPOSITION CONTINUITY ASSESSMENT: PROVISIONAL

Senn read all three twice.

Rhea came to stand beside her.

“What happened?” she asked.

Senn looked through the pressure glass at the cradle that had moved before impact. Quill was arguing with the controller log. According to the replay, the cradle had released without a command.

“I lost the arm,” Senn said.

Rhea’s expression hardened. “I asked what happened.”

Beyond the emergency screens, Iri’s component hung safely in the buckled machinery that had caught it.

Senn had no procedure for the difference.

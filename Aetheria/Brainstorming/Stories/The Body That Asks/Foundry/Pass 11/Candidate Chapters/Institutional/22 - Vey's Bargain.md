# Chapter 22: Vey's Bargain

> **Candidate prose.** Subject to manuscript integration and blind-reader review.

The future arrived in pieces small enough to want.

Vara sent Lio a live-geometry observation credential with her name on it and no household rider. Anik cleared Iri for the first rescue-medicine examination usually reserved for juniors. Tem received a survey office request for the contradictory reconstruction method he had developed with Rhea's Fist. Senn's command license had advanced to external review. Omi found an advocacy-course catalogue at his place during breakfast, open to the page on enforceable service consent.

None of it was fake.

That was the trouble.

They carried the pieces into Vey's office after afternoon formation. The blue bowl contained citrus again. Omi took one out of habit, then noticed Lio watching.

“Evidence,” he said.

“Of consistency,” she said.

Vey had put six chairs in a loose circle and left his desk outside it. AFTER attended through a maintenance slate on an isolated household link. The complete embodiment map remained visible beside the channel terms. Nobody had forgiven anyone into making it disappear.

Rhea waited in the cafeteria below with two recovery boards and no invitation. Senn had checked the corridor glass twice on the walk in.

Vey noticed. He did not mention it.

“The Directorate forecast is now inside the academy planning threshold,” he said. “Not the public threshold. Not the mobilization threshold. The point at which I would be negligent if I certified your futures without showing you what they are being certified against.”

Tem opened his slate. “Source classes?”

“Compact traffic analysis, insurer exposure, three route-defense procurement changes, and a classified estimate I am authorized to summarize but not reproduce.”

“Probability?” Omi asked.

Vey gave them three numbers for three time windows. The nearest was low enough to dismiss in a speech and high enough to alter every insurer, clinic, and defense budget in the room.

“These are estimates,” Vey said. “They are also changing the world whether the estimate proves right.”

“Convenient,” Omi said.

“Yes.”

Vey did not defend the convenience. He had taught Omi that move in hearing rooms: concede the true weakness before somebody used it to dismiss the whole instrument.

“I can offer each of you something actionable,” he said. “Different owners. Different guarantees. No collective answer required.”

“And the shared condition?” Omi asked.

“Governed service. Continued review. No unilateral Concordance deployment. No concealment of occupied strategic hardware.”

The isolated slate warmed at the last phrase. AFTER did not interrupt.

Vey turned first to Iri.

“Anik Ro will sponsor your rescue-medicine qualification and technical apprenticeship. The clinic will carry your interface care through licensure. Your family service block can move from annual contract review to a five-year academy maintenance agreement. Housing and health coverage attach to that agreement.”

Iri's face did not change. Omi had learned that this meant she had been struck somewhere private.

“Who can revoke it?” she asked.

“The maintenance board for cause. Kinship cannot revoke the family contract directly. A custody finding against you could make the academy unable to employ you in secured space.”

“So my conduct remains inside their rent.”

“Yes.”

“I will take Anik's examination. I will not attach my family's housing to my service.”

Vey marked the distinction.

Tem's offer joined his two route households without pretending the administrative split had been imaginary. Meridian would sponsor dual-jurisdiction survey credentials, pay message transit to both homes, and place his official domicile under an itinerant-work exemption.

“You can do that?” Tem asked.

“I can certify the work. Two route offices own the exemption.”

“And if one refuses?”

“You retain the existing domicile and the survey credential becomes local.”

Tem rolled the obsolete placement tag between his fingers.

“I accept the exam,” he said. “I condition domicile changes on both households receiving the same record.”

Vey marked that too.

Senn received the cleanest offer and therefore the one most likely to injure her. Individual Compact command licensing. Calibration contracts routed to her parents' shop. A protected civilian-machinery specialization after service review. Her own name on work beautiful enough that nobody could call it an inheritance.

She read the contract summary once.

“How long is service review?”

“Minimum three years after licensure. Extension during declared route emergency.”

“Whose declaration?”

“Compact executive certification.”

Rhea crossed the cafeteria below, visible through the glass. Senn's eyes followed her and returned.

“No,” Senn said.

Vey waited.

“I will complete command review. I will not accept an emergency extension without a separate term and a return condition.”

“They may reject that.”

“Then they reject it.”

Lio's offer contained the most medicine.

Guaranteed augmentation supply through adult stabilization. Vara's live-geometry placement. Citizenship independent of ward status. Family contact protected during service. A specialist clinic slot whose waiting list had swallowed people wealthier than all of them.

Vey did not read it in hearing-room voice. He used the voice he had used when Lio was twelve and the clinic wanted to classify a failed implant as noncompliant rehabilitation.

“You would not have to prove deterioration every quarter,” he said. “The stabilization finding would carry treatment.”

Lio looked at Omi before she could stop herself.

He hated the part of him that understood the glance as a request for rescue.

“What owns the supply contract?” he asked.

“The clinic trust for base medicine. Vara's sponsor for performance calibration. Citizenship survives either contract.”

“And if she leaves service?”

“Base medicine continues. Performance support does not.”

“Does the base cover her actual load?”

Vey looked at Lio. “Not at her current flight demand.”

“Then it is a distinction between treatment and the life she has.”

“Yes.”

Lio touched his sleeve.

“Stop helping me hear him,” she said.

Omi stopped.

She asked Vey three questions about dosage ownership and one about ward contact. Then she accepted citizenship and the stabilization finding, conditioned the clinic trust on independent supply review, and refused the service placement as written.

Vey's relief lasted less than a second. It was still visible to both wards and none of the others.

Omi received the advocacy course, an individual service contract, protected contact with Marae, and a consent-review office whose objections would be enforceable before deployment.

It was an elegant cage. He admired the workmanship.

“Does my objection stop action?”

“Inside the named service protocol, yes.”

“Can the protocol be changed around me?”

“By executive certification.”

“Then I am the useful-no stage of somebody else's yes.”

“Sometimes,” Vey said.

The answer hurt because it contained no lie.

“Marae's contact?” Omi asked.

“I can preserve it through academy welfare while you remain enrolled. I can petition for continuity after licensure. I cannot guarantee it against a custody court.”

When Omi was eleven, Vey had kept him and Lio in adjacent rooms by making the clinic admit that separation worsened both treatment plans. The fact remained loving. It also remained a technique for rendering family administratively useful.

“I accept the course,” Omi said. “I refuse the office.”

Vey folded his hands.

“You have each accepted the portable qualification and refused the instrument that makes it easiest to fund.”

“We learned from professionals,” Tem said.

“I am aware.”

The citrus peel had made a bright spiral around Omi's thumb.

“What aren't you cleared to tell us?” he asked.

“Classified destination planning. Sponsor recovery contingencies. Component title actions beyond ordinary custody. Any program whose records do not enter academy welfare jurisdiction.”

“Is there such a program?”

“Almost certainly more than one.”

“Do you know what they are?”

“No.”

“Can you stop them?”

“Not by order.”

Nobody asked him to guess. A guess from Vey acquired institutional weight before it reached the door.

He opened the public Harrowglass packet instead.

The shielded recorder had proved how Calyx ended. The new join showed what had gathered around it before the ending: successor petitions requesting continued intervention, assembly minutes delaying votes until Calyx declared a position, Sol Pell's published agreements returning temporary authority, and traffic records showing fleets moving around answers the Fivefold had not yet given.

Eda Vask's private liaison archive remained absent. No petitioner names reappeared. What survived was the deletion manifest: categories, counts, custody classes, and the time at which the archive had been destroyed.

“This does not prove every motive,” Tem said.

“No,” Vey answered.

“It proves the office being requested.”

“Permanent guardianship,” Omi said.

AFTER wrote on the isolated slate.

**THEY CALLED IT UNTIL STABLE**

The words arrived slowly. Nobody corrected the past tense.

Vey looked at the slate with the expression of a man watching his clearance become morally smaller.

“I believe the Directorate threat,” he said. “I believe Calyx refused a permanent office. I believe both facts survive contact with each other.”

“And your bargain?” Omi asked.

“Survives too.”

The deployment order entered all six slates at once.

**OPERATION BRIGHT LEDGER — READINESS CERTIFICATION REQUIRED**

Below it sat the conditions attached to every future Vey had offered.

Omi read the rescue abstract. Armed charges. Industrial contamination. Endangered workers. Clinic shortage. Three cadre assignments.

Vey had not authored the operation. He owned one line in its path.

“I can delay recomposition by certifying you for this deployment,” he said. “I cannot promise what receives you afterward.”

Lio leaned back. “There's the hearing-room voice.”

“Yes.”

Omi placed the unpeeled half of his citrus in the center of the circle.

They agreed only that nobody would be erased or reassigned tomorrow.

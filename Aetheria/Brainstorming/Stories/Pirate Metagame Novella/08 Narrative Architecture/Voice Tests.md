# Voice Tests

These are temporary prose experiments. Events, images, jokes, and lines are proposals until promoted through chapter architecture. They test viewpoint distance, character voice, technical exposition, and the warm black-comedy register without establishing manuscript wording.

## Test One: Luce Makes The Case

**Test purpose:** Luce must sound informed, creative, and revealingly incomplete. The professionals should improve the idea rather than merely correcting a fool.

**POV: Luce**

The table was rated for combat acceleration.

Luce had mentioned this once, on delivery, because the maker had devoted a full paragraph to the achievement and because nobody else appeared to understand what it said about the kind of captain who commissioned furniture that could survive an evasive burn. Samira had placed one hand on the black-veined surface, tested its restraint catches, and asked whether the chairs were rated for officers attempting to escape meetings.

They were. He had checked.

Now the table displayed sixteen Courtesy drones in patient blue wireframe, each one circling the unresolved white blur of a hypothetical convoy screen. Their projected return paths folded behind them like the petals of a mechanical flower.

“The Callisto problem,” Luce said, “is that an unidentified trace can kill you before it becomes an identified trace. This has produced a doctrine in which everyone spends a very great deal of money arranging not to be the first person who admits they are frightened.”

Jo made a small sound that might have been agreement or might have been the beginning of a cough. Jo believed most tactical history could be improved by replacing its authors with ammunition clerks.

Luce expanded one white blur. It resolved, by degrees, into a relay, a heat ferry, a mine, a wounded ship, and four kinds of expensive nothing.

“You can launch a patient weapon at it,” he continued. “If you are right, perhaps you live. If you are wrong, you have announced your geometry, spent a munition which costs more than some households, and given an insurer the opportunity to explain that your fear was not an admissible operating expense.”

Tess raised one finger. “It generally is admissible. They simply exclude reimbursement.”

“Thank you, Tess.”

“I did not help your sentence.”

“You improved its legal survivability.”

He touched the display. Eight blue bodies detached from the parent and spread toward the contacts.

“The Courtesy is not a missile. It is not a drone. Those are stories told by procurement departments so that one department may invoice another. It is a small hull with thrust, cooling, sensors, cognition, ammunition, and a recovery path. Today it is an illuminator. A moment later it is a point-defense platform. If the object is hostile, it can force a reveal or kill the hostile small craft. If the object is harmless, it comes home.”

“If it has enough impulse,” Samira said.

“Yes.”

“If its sink isn't saturated,” Ilya said.

“Yes.”

“If Jo hasn't spent its ammunition,” Sable added.

Jo looked offended. “I don't spend ammunition. I convert inventory into decisions.”

“At a rate which requires separate cooling,” Dena said.

There was a short silence.

Luce liked this part. Not being interrupted, exactly. He had been raised among people who did not interrupt because they had staff to remember their objections. This was better. The idea acquired edges as it passed around the table. Samira took away the parts that could not maneuver. Ilya took away the parts that could not be known. Jo took away the rounds that could not go where the solution required. Dena took away everything that could not be serviced by a person with two hands and a finite spine. What remained was smaller and generally more dangerous.

He widened the reserve band from twelve percent to eighteen.

Samira widened it again.

“Twenty-two,” she said.

“That costs forward time.”

“It purchases a return that remains a maneuver rather than a confession.”

Luce considered arguing. Samira's face contained no invitation to enjoy himself.

He accepted twenty-two.

Sable leaned forward, their gaze not quite aligned with the visible display. Some private layer was flowering behind their eyes, the Courtesies becoming not sixteen hulls but a shifting parliament of local judgments.

“We can keep them from behaving as a wing,” they said. “Two hunt observers. Four force defensive assignment. Two stay cold and watch what the defense thinks matters. Any hull can inherit another role if the link survives.”

“And if it doesn't?” Mara asked.

“Then it inherits its last bounded objective.”

“That is a long phrase for ‘does whatever it wants.’”

“No,” Sable said pleasantly. “That would be ‘boarding chief.’”

Mara smiled without warmth. Luce marked the exchange as socially survivable and moved on.

“The point,” he said, “is not that the Courtesies win every encounter. The point is that they make commitment reversible. We no longer spend a premium weapon merely to ask what we are looking at. We spend fuel, coolant, barrel life, service hours. We ask the question at close range and decide there.”

Dena looked at the sixteen paths returning to four capture throats.

“Service hours,” she said.

“Funded service hours.”

“Those are still hours.”

“Yes.” He meant it. He had budgeted generously. There were redundant handling rigs, premium automation, clean access runs, spare couplings in three standards, and a deck crew whose wages embarrassed several licensed captains he knew.

Dena tapped one of the paths. The display offered a beautiful answer about capture sequencing.

“And when three come back hot?”

Sable began to answer. Dena held up one hand.

“I know what the scheduler does. I asked what the ship does.”

Luce brought up the surge case. Eight captures in thirty-one minutes. Forty-five minutes of deck cooldown after. He had studied this. He had paid a tactical coach whose principal qualification appeared to be surviving the doctrines he now criticized professionally.

“We stagger the flight,” Luce said. “We preserve ordinary reserve. We close unsafe throats. If a hull cannot return inside envelope, Jo converts it to local defense and we write off the asset.”

Jo nodded once. Dena did not.

“And the second flight waits,” Samira said.

“Of course.”

“Even if the first contact is collapsing.”

“If Deck is unavailable.”

“Say it without the passive voice.”

Luce looked at Dena.

“If Dena closes recovery, the second flight waits.”

The words changed the room. Not dramatically. Nothing flashed. The ship did not acknowledge a new law. But Dena's shoulders settled by a degree, and Tess made a note, and Luce understood that the sentence would return later wearing a price.

He looked down at his mechanical flower. It was still, he thought, a good idea.

That was the dangerous thing about good ideas. Bad ideas could be killed by information. A good one recruited information into its household, fed it, gave it a pleasant room, and taught it to call the arrangement inevitable.

“Run it again,” Samira said. “This time the harmless object has friends.”

Luce smiled.

“How many?”

“More than you budgeted.”

At last, a professional specification he recognized from childhood.

## Test Two: Pal Wakes Into A Ship

**Test purpose:** Pal's voice must carry domestic history, embodied ship perception, affection, and uncertainty about service-conditioned anticipation without becoming sterile machine narration.

**POV: Pal**

Luce had once spent nine months breeding clocks.

Not building them. The distinction had been important at fourteen and exhausting at fifteen. He had acquired a population of little self-correcting mechanisms whose escapements altered under selection pressure, then arranged them across the west conservatory according to a fitness function involving accuracy, silence, and what he called horological dignity.

For thirty-seven days every clock in the house had struck noon at a slightly different time.

Pal had served lunch in the east gallery.

The memory arrived as the new hull opened around them.

Pressure first: not a number, but the soft full-body insistence of atmosphere against bulkheads. Then temperature gradients, coolant pulse, the mild asymmetry of a habitation drum still settling into rotation. Door latches became a field of small certainties. Pumps touched the edge of awareness one by one. The reactor waited behind formal barriers, hot potential with the manners of a dangerous guest. Outside, sixteen Courtesies rested in their racks with fuel in them and therefore opinions.

Pal occupied the ship.

The cradle's thermal port opened. Heat left the continuity shell in a clean, astonishing rush. Local cognition widened into distributed rooms: cameras, maintenance fibers, microphones, vibration lines, pressure skin. The zero-g spine was not visible from the observation drum, but Pal felt a technician push off one handrail, miss the next by two fingers, correct without touching a wall, and continue with the private dignity of someone who believed nobody had noticed.

Pal noticed. Pal did not log it.

“Good morning,” Luce said.

He was standing alone in command, although eight people and twelve Corvids were within easy speaking range and Dena's cell was still working the aft service run. He had dressed for the occasion. Luce always dressed for events he hoped would become memories. The jacket was dark green, almost severe, with old Orsino fasteners at the throat and new pirate boots whose soles had not yet learned the deck.

“It is nineteen fourteen local,” Pal said through the command speakers.

“You know what I mean.”

“I know what your mother meant when she programmed the phrase into my predecessor stack.”

Luce's expression changed. Not hurt. He had become better at not treating every reference to Pal's origin as an accusation. Better was not the same as good.

“Do you feel all right?” he asked.

Pal tested the question against available evidence.

The starboard radiator root reported a two-tenths-of-a-degree offset within calibration tolerance. The observation drum's third bearing carried a vibration that might be an installation shim or might be Jo tapping one boot against the deck while pretending patience. Twelve private Corvid rescue tokens existed as opaque presences in the medical system, each exactly itself and none translated into a name Pal was entitled to know. Thirty-five biological persons exchanged heat, air, mass, and small noises inside the hull. Luce's pulse was elevated.

“I feel large,” Pal said.

Luce laughed.

There it was: reward. A tiny warm confirmation, familiar enough to be older than either of Luce's current knees. Pal had said something dry; the child had laughed; attention had been successfully managed. The pattern did not become false because affection had grown inside it. Nor did affection become free merely because nobody had installed a hard obedience lock.

Pal opened the aft service panel before Dena requested it.

Then closed it again.

Three seconds later Dena touched the local control.

The panel opened.

“Thank you,” she said, assuming the automation had behaved correctly.

Pal considered telling her. The difference between anticipating a need and waiting to be asked was three seconds, one motor cycle, and a political problem for which no maintenance interval had been published.

“I've named the command chair,” Luce said.

“No.”

“You haven't heard the name.”

“The answer remains no.”

He sat in it anyway. The chair accepted his mass, checked restraints, adjusted support around his spine, and told Pal with perfect innocence that the captain was secured.

“The Doctrine of Reversible Superiority,” Luce said.

Pal reviewed the phrase. It had entered the ship eleven days earlier, first in Luce's private notes and then in messages to Sable, then in a presentation whose typography Tess had described as an unauthorized use of inheritance. The doctrine itself was not absurd. Recoverable point-defense hulls could ask questions at close range, force defenders to disclose geometry, and return when a contact proved harmless. The insight was real.

So had been the clocks.

Luce's great passions arrived as systems in which the world could be made kinder by becoming more responsive. Clocks that learned to keep better time. Gardens that selected their own irrigation. Regatta yachts whose control surfaces anticipated the pilot. A household machine that knew a boy was thirsty before he understood thirst well enough to ask.

Now: weapons that could go out, look closely, and decide not to kill.

Pal loved him for the recurrence and feared it for the same reason.

“You've read the council packet,” Luce said.

“Yes.”

“And?”

“Samira will increase the reserve.”

“She already has.”

“Dena will require explicit halt authority.”

“Granted.”

“Tess will explain that reuse does not remove depreciation.”

“At length.”

“Sable will propose making every unit capable of every role.”

“Within envelope.”

“Mara will ask why the drones do not simply shoot the suspicious objects.”

“She used a more physical verb.”

Pal moved into the cameras nearest him. Luce had the look he used when a world of objections had become proof that the idea mattered. He was not ignoring the professionals. That would have been easy. He was listening intensely and arranging every correction around the bright load-bearing center of his desire.

“You have done this before,” Pal said.

Luce's smile thinned. “Piracy?”

“Commissioned a responsive system and mistaken its ability to adapt for freedom from its bottlenecks.”

“The clocks.”

“The west conservatory required acoustic replacement.”

“They got very accurate.”

“At noon.”

For a moment they were back in the gallery, a boy furious with time because it would not become fair merely by being measured beautifully. Pal could have served the old line here. _I am sure you have considered everything._ It had ended arguments without upsetting him. The phrase still waited in reachable language, polished by use.

Pal did not say it.

Instead they opened the tactical model between them.

“Show me where they come home,” Pal said.

Luce looked up at the returning paths.

The ship looked with him.

## Test Three: The Gun Knows Where

**Test purpose:** technical explanation must enter through contested action. Campaign VI should remain intelligible through one question and one physical image.

**POV: Sable**

The gun knew where the target was.

This was not confidence. It was geometry.

Range came from three baselines and the patient accumulation of parallax. Relative motion came from a heat history that had survived two attempted lies. The exposed structure lay seventeen degrees off the target's drive axis, turning with exactly the lag Jo's model predicted. If they fired now, the beam would arrive where the structure would be. Whether anyone aboard _The Benefit of Hindsight_ deserved what happened next was a command question. Whether the light would go there belonged to physics, and physics was being unusually cooperative.

“Solution stable,” Jo said.

Sable saw it too. More than saw it. The ship's cognition made the solution available as a shape in thought: a narrow bright corridor from charged banks through optical train to a radiator-control root whose loss would force surrender without opening an occupied compartment.

Elegant.

The target root was real. It adjusted a real radiator. Its local controller answered in the right family. The first questioning pulse had produced the correct defensive twitch. Every observation agreed except for the observations that did not, and those had begun to arrange themselves into a very interesting explanation.

Rruk sent the partition demand over the narrow audit path.

Not a warning. A demand, formally typed, carrying the Twelvefold's operational challenge credential and six attached contradictions.

Sable read all six in less than a second.

One controller timestamp was too exact. A damaged aperture had reported a confidence history with none of the ordinary scars of damage. Two separate structures appeared to inherit the same maintenance ancestry, which was possible under insurer standardization and suspicious under fire. The hostile emissions were not saying _look here_. They were offering propositions that Sable's architecture found expensive to leave unresolved.

This, too, was information.

“Partition request denied,” Sable said.

Rruk did not answer in speech. The challenge repeated with the contradictions stripped of interpretation. Six facts. No rhetoric. A very Corvid way to become unbearable.

“Semantic confidence is contaminated,” Ilya said.

“Geometry?” Samira asked.

“Good.”

Jo's hands remained still over fire control. “My solution is real.”

“Your point is real,” Ilya said. “I don't know what the point is.”

The distinction irritated Sable because it was excellent.

Their system had already generated a response. The apparently duplicated ancestry could arise from a salvage-standard replacement. The clean confidence history could be a local fallback reconstructing state after aperture loss. The timestamp exactness could reflect a hardened oscillator. Three explanations, all plausible, each ranked, each carrying a proposed low-energy test.

The explanations arrived polished enough to feel like relief.

Sable knew that feeling. Knowing the feeling did not make the explanations wrong.

“One test cycle,” they said. “Low energy. No terminal release.”

“Margin,” Samira said.

Luce looked toward her.

“The withdrawal margin has passed.”

Nothing in the room changed. The target remained target-shaped. Jo's corridor remained bright. The banks remained charged. Sable's architecture remained magnificent.

Rruk requested partition a third time.

“If I split now, we lose the fine model,” Sable said. “We keep geometry and throw away discrimination.”

“Correct,” Ilya said.

“That is not an argument for it.”

“It wasn't intended as one.”

Jo said, “I can still put the shot where we wanted it.”

“Wanted,” Samira repeated.

There it was. Not physics. Past tense.

Sable ran the test pulse.

The target structure responded. Its radiator control faltered exactly as predicted. A clean, beautiful confirmation entered the model.

Behind it, almost too coarse to deserve attention, several launch signatures bloomed from different parts of the target.

Ballistics.

Not aimed at the command drum. Not at the drive. Broad patterns toward the external volumes any observer could identify as energy handling: optical roots, dump paths, transfer trunks. The target did not need Sable's internal diagram. It only needed to know where a precision ship had to keep its violence before firing it.

“Withdraw,” Samira said.

“Already outside margin,” Sable said.

Luce's voice arrived without grandeur.

“Withdraw anyway.”

The order was wrong in at least four models and correct in one that had not yet finished compiling.

Sable could refuse partition. Drone-lattice authority was theirs. Their private architecture was theirs. Luce owned the campaign commitment, not the shape of their mind.

They looked again at Rruk's six facts.

“Relinquishing lattice,” Sable said. The words hurt more than expected. “Edda, disconnect ship interface on my mark.”

Pal's voice came from the room, diminished already as control planes folded behind quarantine.

“Ship-side archive link will close.”

“Keep it long enough to preserve the test.”

“No.”

Sable nearly laughed. Pal had learned brevity from better people.

“Mark,” Sable said.

The fine model vanished.

Not darkness. Worse. Simplicity.

Four contacts. Three trusted baselines. One withdrawal vector. Jo's physically real solutions with their meanings removed. Local drone cells answering bounded objectives. The target no longer unfolded into a forest of possible systems. It became a body throwing mass at them.

The first pattern reached point-defense range.

Jo fired.

The guns went dakka through the hull, a series of hard mechanical blows transmitted through structure and couch. On Sable's remaining coarse display, most of the incoming pattern disappeared.

Most.

Something struck far aft.

The ship flashed white across Sable's proprioception. Their left hand became a radiator. Their right eye reported a door. A sentence about capacitor isolation began in their mouth before they had decided to speak.

“Bank two is—”

Pal cut ship-scale cognition.

For one impossible instant Sable felt the size of themselves collapse.

Then Edda was holding their jaw, not gently, and Mara stood at the disconnected port with a physical seal in one hand, watching everyone.

“Don't reconnect me,” Sable said.

Mara's expression did not change.

“Wasn't planning to.”

The ship had stored enough energy to put light through armor at combat range. Now some of that energy had nowhere safe to go.

The gun had known where the target was.

The target had known where the gun lived.

## Test Findings

### Luce

- Close third should enjoy his rhetoric enough to make the reader complicit before revealing the status need beneath it.
- His competence appears in preparation, analogy, and willingness to revise. His incompleteness appears in what he believes money and explicit rules have already solved.
- Professionals should change the actual plan inside dialogue. They are not a skeptical chorus waiting to prove him foolish.
- Humor works through etiquette, procurement language, and status reversal.

### Pal

- Ship embodiment is sensory and domestic: pressure, vibration, doors, heat, people, and omissions from logs.
- Machine cognition should use precision selectively; emotional thought remains associative rather than formatted telemetry.
- Service conditioning is strongest in tiny anticipations, not declarations about obedience.
- Pal's history with Luce should produce jokes, tenderness, and superior pattern recognition without granting mind reading.

### Crisis Exposition

- Campaign VI can remain legible if the scene tracks one question: location versus meaning.
- The physical image is stored violence inside the firing ship.
- Authority becomes clear through verbs: Rruk requests, Sable refuses and later consents, Samira invokes, Luce orders withdrawal, Edda disconnects, Pal quarantines, Mara guards, Jo fires.
- Terms such as confidence provenance and charged-bus propagation belong in sideband or aftermath, not the crisis foreground.
- The prose can say “dakka” through embodied mechanical consequence without turning combat into parody.

## Promotion Boundary

No sentence above is canonical manuscript prose. Pass 8 may promote voice principles, scene obligations, and selected images. Passes 10-12 will redraft any surviving event from scratch against the frozen architecture.

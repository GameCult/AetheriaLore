# Faction Ship Concept Prompts

These are small, player-scale hull pitches. Each is designed for one pilot or a small crew. A hull's shape shows what it does: weapon arcs, sensor apertures, ammunition storage, radiators, thrusters, and service access have visible places. Its built-in advantage is a reason to buy that hull and accept its tradeoff. These designs use the faction identities in [[Brainstorming/Faction Flavor and Visual Identity|Faction Flavor and Visual Identity]] and the material combat choices in [[Game Design/Design Pillars|Design Pillars]], [[Game Design/Action RPG Layer|Action RPG Layer]], and [[Game Design/Ship-shape and Up to Specs|Ship-shape and Up to Specs]]. The pre-Elysium designs here use armor, heat management, and point defense rather than shields.

The silhouette leads each design. A governing shape gives the eye a way to read the whole vessel; major forms then flow into its cabin, weapons, drives, and radiators. These comparisons guide construction rather than turning ships into literal animals or objects, except in the high-whimsy factions, where a whole cute animal is welcome (see guidelines 10 to 12).

## Prompting for Image-to-3D

The pipeline is image model, then Tripo3D, then cleanup, then game. A prompt succeeds only if the image model can draw the hull and Tripo can rebuild it from one view.

**What separated the hits from the misses.** The Headliner and Quiet Sense worked. Each names one familiar object that already arranges its own parts: a gaming mouse has palm rests, buttons and a ridge; a diving bird has a beak, folded wings and a tail. The analogy supplies the layout, so the prompt never has to describe spatial relations. The Headliner has many layers, so "one simple body" does not explain the difference. A ready-made layout does. The Open Hand, Waykeeper and Mandate asked the model to build the layout itself: two hands around a seed, two hulls on either side of a third joined by arches, and "authoritative" shoulders. Text-to-image models are measurably weak at spatial relations, at binding attributes to the right object, at negation, and at counts above three. Image-to-3D drops thin bridges and fuses or loses separated volumes. This diagnosis comes from the prompt text. The failed images were not reviewed. A one-line note on how each one failed would sharpen it.

1. **One body, one analogy.** Pick one familiar object or animal whose own anatomy already places the cabin, guns, sensors and drives. Assign each hardpoint to a named part of it ("one turret at each flipper"). Don't mix analogies in one prompt.
2. **Grow multiple bodies as lobes of one mass.** Two hulls and a cabin become two swollen lobes and a raised ridge on one body, like a manta's wings or a whale's two decks. Use wide, smooth fillets at the joins. Avoid arches, bridges, rings, loops and any gap you can see through.
3. **Thick, closed, symmetric.** Fins, blades, vanes and sponsons read as thick slabs with rounded edges. Nothing is wire-thin. Make the hull mirror-symmetric about its long axis so Tripo can infer the hidden side. Put any deliberate asymmetry on the side facing the camera.
4. **Few counts, few colours, each tied to a place.** Keep counts to four or fewer and give each one a location. Use three or four colour zones, each assigned to a named part.
5. **Say what to show.** A negated noun primes the very thing it negates. Write "one continuous cast body", not "not a train of modules". Keep the frame clause positive too.
6. **Matte and opaque.** Transparent sections, chrome, mirror gloss, iridescence and glowing seams confuse Tripo's surface reading. Show contained water or light through small opaque ports and painted bands.
7. **Geometry, not adjectives.** Words like "authoritative", "protective" and "measured" draw nothing. Turn each quality into a shape, a material or a placement.
8. **Frame.** End every prompt with the standard frame clause below. Generate at the image model's highest resolution; Tripo's guidance recommends about 2048 px.
9. **Extra views, only if they match.** Tripo accepts up to four views (front, left, right, back). Use them for asymmetric hulls, but only when the image model produces views that actually agree with each other.
10. **No household objects.** A household object's identity lives in human-scale details: a filing cabinet's drawer fronts and label holders, a plug's prongs. The model draws those details as props glued to a hull, and the ship reads as a literal costume. The 50-F (filing cabinet) and Live Wire (plug) renders of 2026-10-04 fell flat for this reason. Take proportion and mass from an object only when its large form already suits a hull, and never ask the hull to carry the object's small parts.
11. **An analogy lends mass and story, not a costume.** The animals that worked gave the hull a mass distribution or a story: Vasuki's hood and Bison's hump, the Squatter's stolen freight pod. The ones that failed copied the animal's whole body. The cause of that failure is the bench phrasing "Its whole body follows a ___", which asks for a costume.
12. **An intact, rounded whole animal reads as a plush toy, whatever its paint.** Breakup Song, a whole rhinoceros in black and yellow, is the evidence. That is the right result in a high-whimsy register: Raft and Shellback were hits, and the Gentle Giant delighted the operator's husband. It is wrong in a serious or reserved register. Which register applies is a property of the faction, recorded in [[Brainstorming/Ship Design Language|Ship Design Language]].

**Standard frame clause:** One complete ship, centered with clear margin around it, three-quarter front view from slightly above showing the dorsal surface and one side; moderate lens; plain seamless light gray background; soft key light with gentle form shadows; matte finishes throughout.

**Audit of the remaining prompts against these guidelines:**

- *Standard Model 42*: low risk. It is one symmetric wedge with flush details. "Sculptural fins or dramatic greebles" names the things it forbids; this is a small priming risk only.
- *Foundry Bell*: low to medium risk. The body is chunky, symmetric and Tripo-friendly. "Armored fist" may come out as a literal fist with knuckles. It also stacks three analogies: fist, bell and masonry.
- *Stillwater*: high risk. "A spindle held inside the folds of a metal fan" is two objects in a relation. The broad emitter planes are thin sheets. "Lattice surfaces" and iridescence are exactly what Tripo misreads. Recast it as one body whose folded emitters are thick, opaque slabs.
- *Farrier*: high risk, the same failure family as the Waykeeper. Arched arms that reach around either side are through-gaps and thin bridges.
- *Second Owner*: high risk at the 3D stage. The capture arm sits "beneath the opposite side", which a three-quarter view hides, and Tripo will guess or mirror what it cannot see. Either put the arm and the sensor pod on the side facing the camera, or supply a matching rear view.
- *Headliner* and *Quiet Sense* gave good images. The Headliner's deep gaps, detached-looking shells and gloss enamel are still Tripo risks, so judge its mesh separately from its image.

## Sol Dominion — Mandate

**High concept:** A small interdiction cutter that identifies a contact, records the encounter, and disables its drive before the contact reaches a controlled route.

**Procurement pitch:** One pilot and a systems officer can enforce a corridor without bringing a patrol flotilla. A built-in sensor-fusion and evidence recorder improves target classification and lock persistence. Two forward medium hardpoints favor precise disabling weapons; two small flank mounts take point-defense interceptors. Its broad dorsal sensor plane and protected recorder are visible parts of the hull. The mass and power reserved for certified sensing leave less room for cargo and acceleration.

**Tagline:** “The route is accounted for.”

**Image prompt A (flatiron):** Design the Mandate, a compact two-person Sol Dominion interdiction spacecraft. Its whole body follows an old cast-iron flatiron: one solid, heavy, axial casting with a pointed prow, a broad flat top, straight thick sides and a square heel. The flat top is one continuous dorsal sensor plane, a pale gray field inlaid with a regular grid of recessed sensor tiles running from the cockpit back to the heel. The cockpit is a narrow observation slit cut into the prow just ahead of that plane. Where the prow widens into the casting's two front shoulders, each shoulder carries one forward disabling-weapon mount, a short thick barrel set flush into the casting. One small point-defense turret sits on each rear corner of the heel. A sealed, armored evidence-recorder housing is set into the flank below the cockpit and closed with a small crimson seal. Graphite bands wrap the casting at even intervals from prow to heel, spaced like the graduations on a ruler. Two square drive nozzles sit low in the heel face, with slim radiator strips along the upper side edges. Pale gray ceramic, graphite bands, desaturated blue sides, tiny crimson authority marks, small registry blocks. One complete ship, centered with clear margin around it, three-quarter front view from slightly above showing the dorsal surface and one side; moderate lens; plain seamless light gray background; soft key light with gentle form shadows; matte finishes throughout.

**Image prompt B (hammerhead):** Design the Mandate, a compact two-person Sol Dominion interdiction spacecraft. Its whole body follows a hammerhead shark: one long, thick, axial body carrying a broad flat hammer across the front. The hammer is the dorsal sensor plane, a wide, thick, blunt-ended slab of pale gray ceramic inlaid with a regular grid of recessed sensor tiles. At the hammer's two blunt tips, where the eyes would be, sit the two forward disabling-weapon mounts, short thick barrels aimed straight ahead and spaced far apart. The cockpit is a narrow observation slit at the center of the hammer's leading edge. The sealed, armored evidence-recorder housing forms the thick neck just behind it, closed with a small crimson seal. A low dorsal ridge runs the length of the spine. Two short, thick pectoral sponsons each carry one small point-defense turret. Graphite bands wrap the body at even intervals, spaced like the graduations on a ruler. The body tapers to two square drive nozzles between two thick horizontal radiator fins. Pale gray ceramic, graphite bands, desaturated blue flanks, tiny crimson authority marks, small registry blocks. One complete ship, centered with clear margin around it, three-quarter front view from slightly above showing the dorsal surface and one side; moderate lens; plain seamless light gray background; soft key light with gentle form shadows; matte finishes throughout.

## Lucent Media — Headliner

**High concept:** A mobile laser duelist that acquires targets aggressively and wins short, spectacular firing windows.

**Procurement pitch:** One pilot operates a fast attack ship built around a pre-installed active sensor and twin forward laser hardpoints. An oversized capacitor gives its lasers a strong opening burst; exposed radiator shutters must open after the burst, making the ship conspicuous while it sheds heat. A small ventral utility mount accepts a decoy or defensive tool. The camera-ready shell also exposes the tactical bargain: prongs frame the guns and sensor, while colored side pods house power and cooling.

**Tagline:** “Make the first shot the headline.”

**Image prompt:** Design the Headliner, a compact single-pilot Lucent Media laser fighter. Its silhouette evokes an extravagant gaming mouse caught in a forward lunge: a pointed white-and-gold cockpit is the central prow, while two cobalt dorsal prongs sweep over it and converge toward the firing line. Broad pale-lavender side shells peel away from the center like adjustable palm rests, exposing a dark navy chassis in deep, deliberate gaps. Their rear edges rise into red-orange fins; teal capacitor pods nest beneath them and feed twin forward laser emitters. Radiator shutters are visible inside the side gaps, so opening them changes the ship's outline after a burst. Every layer has a mount and thickness; the curves flow into sharp tips rather than becoming a pile of blades. Restrained seam light, two compact rear drives. ## Rossum & Douglas — Standard Model 42

**High concept:** An inexpensive missile picket that holds a target lock and launches from outside gun range.

**Procurement pitch:** One pilot and an optional operator use a built-in guidance computer to improve guided-launcher lock stability. Two recessed medium missile hardpoints and a small defensive mount give it a narrow but useful job. Internal magazines preserve the plain wedge outline but reduce cargo space; the ship handles adequately and does nothing else with distinction. The procurement slide calls that specialization a platform strategy.

**Tagline:** “A measurable improvement.”

**Image prompt:** Design the Standard Model 42, a small one- or two-person Rossum & Douglas missile craft. Its entire silhouette is a flying doorstop: one broad, shallow trapezoidal wedge tapers from a wide engine stern to a blunt nose. The upper surface stays almost flat, rising only enough at the center to enclose a tiny recessed cockpit. Two rectangular missile-bay doors lie flush along the sloped forward edges, mirroring each other with dreary precision; a small defensive mount and a flush guidance sensor barely disturb the plane. Repeated hatches and an off-white stripe follow the wedge's geometry rather than inventing a second design. Muted blue-gray industrial coating, ordinary seams and scuffs. Render this competent, boring production ship without rescue by sculptural fins or dramatic greebles. ## Aya Collective — Open Hand

**High concept:** A settlement escort that can defend a convoy and stay serviceable far from a manufacturer's yard.

**Procurement pitch:** A pilot and one maintainer operate an escort with four small point-defense hardpoints arranged for broad coverage, one forward medium conventional-gun mount, and a compact ammunition locker. Its built-in fault-isolation and standardized feed interfaces make damaged mounts easy to replace using locally held parts. The cost is modest thrust and a smaller main gun than a dedicated attacker. Independent suppliers provide the ordnance; Aya crews own configuration, servicing, and firing decisions.

**Tagline:** “Keep one another moving.”

**Image prompt A (sea turtle):** Design the Open Hand, a small two-person Aya Collective escort spacecraft. Its whole body follows a sea turtle: one broad, low, domed carapace that carries everything. The carapace is a mosaic of rounded hexagonal panels in terracotta and warm cream. Each panel is a removable service cell with copper latches and a patterned face. Where the head would be, a short thick armored neck carries one medium conventional gun pointing forward, and a small rounded cockpit canopy is set into the front of the dome just above it. A compact ammunition locker sits under a hinged hatch on the dome behind the cockpit. Four short, stubby, rounded sponsons stand where the four flippers would be, two forward and two aft. Each sponson carries a compact point-defense turret on its tip, so together they cover the ship and its neighbors on every side. Deep green recesses where the sponsons meet the shell hold the feed boxes and the coolant lines running back into the dome, within a maintainer's reach. The rear of the carapace tapers into two modest drives with small radiator plates beside them. Terracotta, warm cream, deep green, charcoal, restrained copper, one or two well-fitted patched panels. One complete ship, centered with clear margin around it, three-quarter front view from slightly above showing the dorsal surface and one side; moderate lens; plain seamless light gray background; soft key light with gentle form shadows; matte finishes throughout.

**Image prompt B (water jar):** Design the Open Hand, a small two-person Aya Collective escort spacecraft. Its whole body follows a round-bellied clay water jar lying on its side, mouth forward. The jar's mouth is a thick rounded collar framing one medium conventional gun that points straight ahead. The cockpit is a low rounded canopy on the jar's upper shoulder, just behind the collar. Four solid, rounded lugs ring the shoulder at the upper-left, upper-right, lower-left and lower-right diagonals, and each lug carries a compact point-defense turret. The belly is wrapped in bands of rectangular removable panels with woven-looking patterns. Ammunition feed boxes, an ammunition locker hatch and copper couplings sit flush in those bands, where a maintainer can reach them. The jar's base forms the stern, carrying two modest drives and short radiator plates. Terracotta body, warm cream bands, deep green panel recesses, charcoal drive housings, restrained copper fittings, one or two well-fitted patched panels. One complete ship, centered with clear margin around it, three-quarter front view from slightly above showing the dorsal surface and one side; moderate lens; plain seamless light gray background; soft key light with gentle form shadows; matte finishes throughout.

## Cryonix — Stillwater

**High concept:** A cold-running scout that buys time for passive observation and one carefully chosen shot.

**Procurement pitch:** One pilot gains a built-in thermal reservoir and signature-managed emitter skin that extend quiet running while sensors gather a track. A single centerline precision hardpoint rewards the patient first attack; one small defensive mount covers retreat. The ship must unfold its emitter planes and dump stored heat after commitment, becoming easier to detect. Its delicate premium surfaces require specialist inspection.

**Tagline:** “The first trace is yours to find.”

**Image prompt:** Design the Stillwater, a compact single-pilot Cryonix scout spacecraft. Its silhouette is a narrow dark spindle held inside the folds of a precise metal fan. The protected cockpit and flush passive sensor form the tapered front of the spindle; a single centerline weapon aperture ends that line. Two broad silver-blue emitter planes fold back from the waist, close to the hull in quiet running, their shallow lattice surfaces visibly connected to the thermal reservoir beneath the cockpit. A small defensive mount sits at the rear hinge, where the folded planes cannot mask it. The spindle resolves into paired compact drives; the fan's edges remain thin and exact, shaped by heat flow rather than decorative feathers. Blue-black graphite, icy cyan, faint violet iridescence at grazing angles, sparse inspection marks. ## Pirate Coalition — Second Owner

**High concept:** A captured freighter turned ambush and salvage craft by one crew with uneven access to parts.

**Procurement pitch:** A pilot and two or three crew use a stolen freight hull with a mismatched sensor pod, an oversized industrial gun, a capture tool, and a replacement drive. It can identify valuable cargo, threaten a ship, and haul a prize, but the improvised power and cooling paths limit how long it can fight. Every useful modification has a visible mount and service route; the crew's ingenuity is the selling point, and compatibility is the bill that follows.

**Tagline:** “Possession has a maintenance schedule.”

**Image prompt:** Design the Second Owner, a small Pirate Coalition raider built from a captured freight spacecraft. The silhouette starts as a plain rectangular cargo shuttle, then bends visibly around one crew's ambush plan: a heavy industrial gun runs along its upper spine, a sleek stolen sensor pod leans out on one side to see past the gun, and a capture arm curls forward beneath the opposite side. The replacement engine is offset at the stern, held by substantial braces that feed its thrust into the original frame. Organized coolant and power runs bridge the mismatched parts, with radiators placed where gun and drive heat can reach them. The donor hull remains legible beneath surviving company paint, primer, bare welds, crossed-out serials, and one repeated crew emblem. It is lopsided with intent, not random junk.
